# Shared fishing sea

Approved 2026-11-05: each player is a separate fishing boat in one common online map. Enter through the hub's “Ra khơi” (see “Hub, sign-in and loading” below); authenticate with the Supabase Email/Password dialog, then sail immediately. No room creation, invitations, lobby, readiness or host. `?view=ocean` restores this direct-entry route after reload. Returning to the hub leaves the sea.

## Implementation and ownership

- `src/app/harbor.ts`: active hub, account observer, entry/exit ownership.
- `src/app/ocean.ts`: controls, fishing interaction, HUD/dialogs, animation and cleanup.
- `src/ocean/model.ts`: deterministic per-UID spawn, bounded movement, island collisions, remote payload validation.
- `src/ocean/babylon-scene.ts`: the Babylon.js scene: one engine, Havok physics for the player's boat, a chase camera, instanced boats for other players and safe text labels. It builds from `water.ts` (wave-displaced sea shader), `sky.ts` (gradient sky, sun, clouds), `boat-visual.ts` and `island-visual.ts` (procedural models merged into one vertex-coloured mesh each, so a boat is one draw call and other players' boats are instances; the land's shape is pure maths in `island-shape.ts`), `shore.ts` (the shore field every coast-aware part measures from) and `swash.ts` (the swash on the beaches), `clear-water.ts` / `clear-water-pass.ts` (the see-through sea, see "Clear water"), `havok.ts` (loads the WASM), `render-scale.ts` (resolution governor) and `sea-style.ts` (shared colours and sun).
- `src/platform/ocean.ts`: loads `ocean-socket.ts`, the Supabase-authenticated Render Socket.IO client. There is no other provider and no fallback: without `VITE_SUPABASE_URL`, `VITE_SUPABASE_PUBLISHABLE_KEY` and `VITE_API_URL` the harbor shows a clear message. `server/ocean.mjs` owns the ephemeral shared sea for that API instance.

The Monopoly board game, its rooms hub and Firebase were removed on 2026-11-16 ([decision 0012](decisions/0012-remove-firebase-and-monopoly.md)); this game is all the repository holds.

## Controls and fishing

On touch, one thumb drags the on-screen stick (`src/ocean/stick.ts`). Touching the stick's own corner (the lower-left 34% wide, 60% high) with a finger makes the ring jump under the thumb and return home on release, so the thumb never has to find it; touching the ring itself keeps it where it is. A mouse only takes the ring itself (`stickGrab` in `src/ocean/input-layer.ts`). There is no title on the sea screen (the “Biển chung” title and its subtitle were removed; the screen keeps `aria-label="Map biển chung"` for assistive technology). good-news notices (“Đã vào biển chung”, “Đã kết nối lại”) fade after 4 s while still being announced to screen readers, and name labels that would overlap are stacked (`separateLabels` in `src/ocean/indicator.ts`). The stick: up/down is throttle (forward/reverse) and left/right is steering, so a diagonal drag runs and turns at once; farther means stronger, a small dead zone ignores a resting thumb, and releasing or cancelling the touch stops the boat. The other thumb is free: “Thả câu” acts on press because browsers do not synthesize a click for a second finger while the first holds the stick. WASD/arrows still work as full deflection, and the stick wins while held. Fishing is described under “Fishing gameplay” below. Position, the fish store and the catch count are saved in this browser's localStorage (key `photyphu:sea:v1:<uid>`, autosave every 5 s, on each catch or release, when the tab is hidden and on leaving) and resumed on return; a save older than 7 days, outside the sea, inside an island or corrupt falls back to the harbor (the fish store is kept). The save is per device, not per account, and is not a reward, currency or anti-cheat-protected economy.

If the connection drops during play the game reconnects by itself (same boat, same place, up to 90 s, status “Mất kết nối. Đang kết nối lại…”). Revoked sessions, a second window taking over, or a full sea are not retried and show the reason with a way back. Other players see whether a boat is fishing.

The sea is drawn even while connecting or offline; boats appear once connected. `index.html` carries a small classic script: if the game has not drawn after 8 seconds it shows the failure reason with “Tải lại” and “Xóa bộ nhớ đệm và tải lại” (unregisters this app’s service worker and shell caches) instead of a blank page.

Foreground controls fit 844×390, 932×430 and 667×375; portrait falls back to stacked actions. Controls have at least 44px targets, dialog focus, keyboard operation and reduced-motion waves. Native phone performance and OS input behavior still require device verification.

**Look of the controls** (variant A, chosen by the user): icon-only glass buttons (translucent white with a blur; a plain translucent fallback where `backdrop-filter` is missing), all at least 48 px: back (arrow), the fish store (fish icon and the count) and the crew count. The fishing button is the one solid, yellow control (78 px) and its icon shows what it does: a hook (cast), an arrow thrown out (let go), a turning reel (haul in), a jolt (pull now); the words are only its accessible name (`aria-label`: Thả câu / Thả ra! / Thu cần / Giật!, the store button reads “Kho cá n”). There is no first-time hint text. On portrait phones the harbor art is shown wide enough for the whole boat and sun instead of being cropped to the middle (`.hub-art` in `style.css`); a faint seam can still be seen at the bottom edge of the art on a 390×844 screen. Seen in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390, 667×375, 390×844 and 360×640 only, not on a real phone.

## Input layer (no long-press menus)

**Minimap and crew list.** A small map of the whole sea sits in the top-right corner under the crew button (`src/app/minimap.ts` draws it, `src/ocean/minimap-math.ts` has the geometry, tested in `tests/minimap.test.mjs`): north up, the sea wall dashed, the islands with a sand ring, the channel buoys, the harbor, the other boats as arrows in their hull colours (a ring when they are fishing) and the player as a yellow arrow with a pulse that points where the boat heads. The still layer is painted once and the boats are redrawn ten times a second; it is only drawn, so no touch goes to it, and it is hidden while the loading card is up. The crew button opens a list (not a plain list of text any more): a header with the title, the number of boats and an X, then one rounded row per boat with a round avatar in its hull colour (`src/ocean/palette.ts` gives the same colour everywhere), the name (shortened with an ellipsis if long), what it is doing (green dot “Đang trên biển”, red dot “Đang câu”) and its distance in metres, you first and then the nearest. Names are put in as text, never as HTML. Checked in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390, 667×375 and 390×844.

The sea's controls (back, crew, fish store, the fishing button, the stick ring) are only **drawn**: they have `pointer-events: none`. One transparent layer (`.ocean-input`, `src/ocean/input-layer.ts`) takes every touch, finds the control under the finger by position (hit area at least 44 px even when the drawing is smaller), keeps one route per finger so the stick and the fishing button work together, and blocks `contextmenu`, `selectstart` and `dragstart`. Because a finger never lands on a button, text, icon or link, the browser has nothing to turn into a long-press menu or callout, and nothing that can cancel a held action. A touch the system cancels counts as a lift, so nothing is left held. The buttons stay real `<button>`s for the keyboard and assistive technology: their `click` still works. While the loading card is up the layer is off and the back button takes its own touches. Dialogs (crew list, fish store) are still HTML and are tap-only. This is verified in Chromium with real touch events and a synthetic `contextmenu`; it has not been tried on a real phone or in Safari.

## Fishing gameplay

Rules are pure code in `src/ocean/fishing.ts` (species, zones, the cast, the fish's behaviour, the store); the screen is `src/app/fishing-ui.ts`; the line, the rod's bend, the fish and the ripples are `src/ocean/fishing-gear.ts` (drawn through `OceanScene.setGear`). Every fish state is a signal in motion, not text.

1. **Charge and throw.** Hold “Thả câu” (or Space) and a power bar fills in 1.4 s; let go to throw. The lure lands 8 to 40 m ahead and a little to starboard (where the rod points). A dotted arc and a ring on the water preview the landing; green means the sweet spot (22 to 30 m), where fish wait less and better fish are likelier. A throw that would land on an island or outside the sea fizzles: while the button is held the bar, the dotted arc and the ring turn red (the ring is drawn over the sand, which would hide it), and on letting go the red bar stays about a second and shakes with the button, so the throw is never just ignored. A quick tap throws the shortest throw (8 m). Keyboard activation without a hold throws at the sweet spot.
2. **Nibbles.** There is no float. The line hangs slack in a curve to the water (a rope, see below). A nibble twitches the line and nods the rod tip, spreads a ring where the line enters the water and shows a blinking “!” above that spot: a fish is about to bite. A round has 1 to 3 nibbles; one fish in five loses interest and another comes later. Pulling now only reels the line in.
3. **The bite.** The line goes taut and trembles, the line is dragged under and away from the boat, the button turns into the pull, a ring around it drains and the screen edge reddens. The pull must come within **1 second** (`HOOK_WINDOW_MS`); after that the fish is gone.
4. **The catch.** The landed fish hangs from the rod tip in 3D (sized by its weight) with a small tag (rarity stars, weight, ✦ for a species record) and two drawn icon buttons: keep (✓, the fish rises away into the store) and put back (the fish falls into the sea). The fish is stored only when kept; pressing the button again keeps it and starts the next throw in the same press (`pressAction` in `src/ocean/cast-rules.ts`: before, that press only kept the fish and was swallowed, so the first throw after every catch seemed dead; see decision 0011, item 14), opening the store or leaving the sea keeps it too, so a landed fish is never lost by accident. There is no card or dialog.
5. **Kho cá.** A full-screen hub (not a small dialog, see "Settings hub" for the shared shell) with the rail's `Đóng` tab (also Esc), the count held, and a grid of only the species held now: picture, name, count (`×n`), record weight with rarity stars and a put-back icon button (one fish back in the sea; the record stays). Nothing is shown for species never caught, no silhouettes. The picture of each species is its own 3D model (the Blender file, see "Sea animal models") drawn once to a small canvas by `src/app/fish-thumb.ts` (maths in `src/ocean/creature-thumb.ts`: turn, shade, sort far to near; no second WebGL context), so the 2D SVG art is gone.

The line is out (and others see the boat as fishing, the boat is not driven) from the throw until the line is in. A boat that drifts more than 48 m from where the line enters the water, a dialog, or a hidden tab reels it in. Ten species are in the table (`SPECIES` in `fishing.ts`: Cá madeka, Cua biển, Sao biển, Sứa, Cá hề, Cá ngựa, Rùa biển, Tôm hùm, Cá heo, Cá mập; the earlier twenty were replaced, see "Sea animal models" and decision 0013). Each has its own 3D model file (`fish-<id>.glb`) and one fight style; which can be caught depends on where the bobber lands: within 30 m of an island's beach, in open water, or more than 128 m from every beach (`zoneAt`, measured from the real coast in `shore.ts`; the ranges were 28 and 125 m from the radius circle, which lies about 2.5 m beyond the sand, so the zones kept their size). Weights are skewed to the light end of each species' range. Everything is simulated on the client with no server authority, so there is no economy, trade or reward; catching and releasing are personal. Not done yet: schools of fish visible on the sea, a fight with line tension, and other players seeing the bobber (they still see the existing short line).

### Tug of war (after the hook)

Pulling inside the one-second window now only **sets the hook** (`hooked`); the fish is landed only after a fight (`src/ocean/fight.ts`, pure rules with their own tests). From then on the button is **held to reel and released to give line**. The fish is in one of three moods: **surge** (it bursts away and takes line: reeling against it spikes the line tension and snaps the line, so let go), **strong** (it pulls steadily and surges now and then: short pulls win line and tire it, reeling too long builds tension until it snaps) and **tired** (it can be reeled in; left alone for a few seconds it recovers). The fish is landed when the distance to the boat runs out (`caught`); a snap, running out the whole line, shaking the hook after 70 s or leaving the sea (`cancel`) is `lost`. Each kind of animal fights in a style (`steady`, `runner`, `heavy`, `jet`, `crawler`, `glider`; the species table picks one) and bigger fish pull harder and last longer. A frame never advances the fight by more than 0.1 s, so a slow phone sees slow motion, not a jump.

Simulated with a bot: a careful player (reel when tired, or when strong and the line is slack) lands every style at light and heavy weights in about 9 to 22 s; holding the button always snaps the line; never reeling never lands the fish. **These numbers are first guesses, not tuned by play**: the constants at the top of `fight.ts` are meant to be adjusted by feel on a real phone. The button exposes `data-phase`, `data-mood`, `data-tension` and `data-distance` for styling and tests (never as text).

**What the fight looks like** (`src/ocean/fight-look.ts` holds the numbers, `fishing-gear.ts` draws them; no text): the line goes white, then yellow, then red as the tension climbs, hums and trembles harder near the snap and sags when the fish tires. The hooked fish is its species' 3D model at the end of the line, `distance` metres from the rod, and it swims **under the water** (see "The hooked fish under the water" below): shallow and thrashing in a surge, deep and steady when strong, rolling on its side near the surface when tired. When the line snaps or the fish gets away the loose end whips back to the rod. Checked with software-rendered screenshots only (844×390): the fish is clearly visible in a surge but small and low when tired and far away; the rod does not bend yet and the button has no tension ring yet. Not verified on a real phone.

**Rod, line and the hand** (no float anywhere). The rod (`src/ocean/rod.ts` for the maths, 4 m long; `src/ocean/rod-plan.ts` for its shape as plain numbers; `src/ocean/rod-visual.ts` for the mesh) is a damped spring on a chunky tapered blank: a fat cork handle with a butt cap, a metal ferrule, four hard bands alternating the boat's own trim and hull colours (so every rod matches its boat), four flat line guides on the underside and a round orange tip, all one small mesh of 304 triangles whose vertices follow the bend; the reel (a round red body with a metal plate, a crank arm with a yellow knob and a stem to the handle, 540 triangles) hangs under the handle facing the stern and never moves. The rod stands in the holder of the Blender boat (its butt is at `ROD_BASE`; `boat.py` `FIT.rodBase` and `rodLean` say the same, and `tests/boat-model.test.mjs` fails if the holder and `ROD_BASE` drift apart). The pull of the line bends it (more near the tip, the length stays the same), it springs back and rings after a release, and a surging fish shakes it. The tip it reports is where the line starts, so the line and the rod always agree; every boat has a rod, only the player's moves. The line (`src/ocean/rope.ts`) is a Verlet rope pinned between the rod tip and the lure or fish's mouth: slack when waiting (it hangs in a curve and rests on the sea), straight and humming when a fish pulls, and its pull on the rod comes from the fight (a surge is hard, a tired fish hardly pulls). The rope's gravity is scaled down as it tightens, since tension swamps its weight. While a fish pulls the line has no spare length at all (a few per cent of spare line sags the middle by metres: 3 % of 20 m is about 2.5 m), and it is drawn pulled onto the straight chord from the rod tip to the fish's mouth, because the simulation lags a fish that swings fast; waiting, it keeps a gentle sag of about half a metre, and a tired fish lets it curve a little. The drawn line is a flat strip that always faces the camera (`src/ocean/ribbon.ts`), thicker with distance (about 2 to 3 px on a phone at any distance: `LINE_WIDTH`), because a GL line is one pixel wide and vanishes, with a dark, dimmer strip 1.7 times wider drawn just behind it (the halo, `PAL.rod.halo`) so the white line shows on bright water and foam as well as dark; it is smoothed (`smoothRope`) so a nearly taut rope is not saw-toothed. The browser test samples the drawn line while a fish pulls and fails if its middle hangs more than half a metre off the straight line. Each ripple stays where it was made, so a running fish leaves a trail of rings. Fish are drawn larger the farther they are (about 2.5 times at 25 m) so they can be seen. The lure is a plump half-metre fish (orange back, cream belly, yellow fins, big eyes, a steel eye on the nose where the line is tied; about 1,000 triangles, lit, built once) pointing its nose at the rod, bobbing, and growing with the distance from the camera (up to 2.4 times) so it is not a speck at 40 m; a small steel hook (60 triangles) hangs under it only while it is in the water, not in flight. A landed fish leaps from the water to hang by the mouth just below the rod tip, its length (1.7 to 3.2 m in the scene) following how heavy it is for its species.

**Device vibration** (`src/app/haptics.ts`, pure and tested with a fake `vibrate`): while the fish surges the phone buzzes without a break, with longer pulses as the line tightens; a taut line near the snap ticks as a warning; a tired fish is quiet; it stops when the fish is landed, lost or the player leaves. It needs `navigator.vibrate`: **Safari on iPhone has none, so iPhones feel nothing**; Android (Chrome) is the only place it can be felt, and I have not felt it on a real phone.

### The hooked fish under the water

While it fights, the fish is below the waterline and is seen through the sea (`src/ocean/underwater.ts` pure maths and GLSL, `underwater-material.ts` the thin GPU part, `fishing-gear.ts` the use). The sea is an opaque shader that writes depth, so a mesh under it is hidden. The fish keeps its true place (about 0.3 to 1.0 m under the wave under it, bobbing with the waves) and is drawn **after** the water in the ordinary transparent queue, with a shader of its own: (1) the vertex shader writes a depth as if each point lay where the view ray crosses a plane 0.5 m above the sea under the fish (squeezed to 5 % toward that point so the parts of the fish keep their order), so the water, which is already in the depth buffer, does not hide it, while a hull or an island in front of the surface still does; (2) the fragment shader computes the depth below the surface at that pixel with the shared wave function (`glslWaveHeight`, the one the sea mesh and the boats use) and discards everything above it, so no part of the fish is ever above the water (`cut` is 0 only while a landed fish leaps out or a released one falls back); (3) below, the colour fades as `exp(-1.2 x depth)`: red goes first (the sea's own absorption), the shading flattens, the colours drain to grey, and it blends into the water colour (deep blue offshore, turquoise in the shallows, `shoal`) and into transparency, so at a metre it is a faint dark shape; past 1.4 m (a fish put back) it fades out. Depth by mood (`moodDepth`, `swimDepth`): surge 0.32 to 0.52 m and thrashing (roll, sway of the body at the tail's beat), strong 0.76 to 1.0 m, steady, tired 0.33 to 0.47 m rolling on its side and now and then belly up; the back is always under the water (the centre is at least 0.2 of the drawn length down) and never below 0.6 of the still water over a shallow floor. Its nose points away from the boat and turns to the side it swings to. The mood, `data-*` attributes and `fight.ts` are unchanged. **The line** goes from the rod tip to the mouth in the water: it is cut where it meets the waves (`surfaceEntry`, found by bisection), the part above is the usual strip, the part below is a strip of its own drawn through the water with the same shader (it stays easier to see than a fish), and small rings (a third of the old size) start at the entry and leave a trail when the fish runs. **Surface**: the dark shadow disc is gone; a V of ripples with a faint boil at its apex lies on the water over the fish's nose when it is close under the surface (`disturbance`: strong for a surge, faint for a tired fish, almost none when it is deep and strong). **Landing**: when it is landed it leaps out as before and hangs in the shared satin skin (the hang pose is unchanged; it keeps the underwater skin only while some of it is still low). **Mirror**: meshes wearing the underwater material are left out of the planar reflection (`reflectsMaterial` in `reflection-model.ts`; they lie under y = 0 and the mirror would draw them floating); the V is named `gear-ring-v` like the rings. **Cost**: no extra pass; per fight the body, the tail, the underwater line strip and the V are four draw calls (the old shadow disc, one, is gone), a fragment shader of about a dozen `sin`/`pow` for the wave height on the pixels of the fish and the strip only, and one shader compile at the first fight (a hitch is possible on a phone). `tests/underwater.test.mjs` pins the rules, the shaders (as text) and, with a headless engine, a fish under the waterline with its nose away from the boat. Seen in software-rendered images (the closer fishing camera, the chase camera and 844 x 390, three moods, a four-frame sequence), not on a phone; the fish is small and faint from the chase camera at 20 m or more, the rings and V carry the reading then. **With the clear-water layer on** (the default, see "Clear water") the fish and the strip of line below the surface are drawn by the layer instead: the fish wears the ordinary satin skin, the special skin above is the fallback for when the layer is off.

## Sea animal models

Every sea animal is a model from the Blender community (BlenderKit), one file per species: `public/models/fish-<species id>.glb`. They replaced the sixteen body plans built from code and recoloured per species (and the generator `tools/blender/fish.py`), see [decision 0013](decisions/0013-community-sea-models.md). The triangle count of each model is kept as it was downloaded; nothing was reduced.

BlenderKit's royalty_free licence allows commercial use but not reselling the model; the game serves these files publicly, so the licence terms still need the owner's own reading. Only the starfish and the crab are CC0. The dolphin download had no material at all, so its colours (blue-grey back, white belly) are painted by `sea_import.py`; the jellyfish takes its emission colours.

**File.** A root `fish` with two children: `body` (one mesh: positions, smooth or flat normals as the model had them, and a final COLOR_0) and `hook` (an empty node at the nose). The nose points to +X, up is +Y, the longest side is 1 and the model is centred on its origin. There is no `tail` node (the models are single meshes, not split for swinging) and no recolouring per species: every colour is baked from the model's own textures, as bytes times 257 in an unsigned-short COLOR_0 (`byte * 257 / 65535`, never one of the old role colours). The export is `tools/blender/sea_import.py`, run in Blender with the models in the scene: it takes each mesh after its modifiers (a Multires level is skipped, an armature is applied at its rest pose, rig bone shapes `WGT-*` are left out), samples the image textures through the UVs itself (the Blender here has no Cycles to bake), turns the model about Z so the nose points to +X, centres it, scales it and writes the GLB itself.

**Sizes.** A species' `lengthCm` is the length of the model's longest side for a 1 kg animal; the drawn length is `lengthCm * cbrt(kg) / 100` m (see `drawnLength`), clamped between 0.3 m and 4 m. The sizes are the real sizes of the animals (a shark 1.5 to 3 m, a clownfish 6 to 12 cm); the small ones are drawn larger than life by the 0.3 m floor. The weight ranges of the six species that already had a price row on the server did not change, so the server needed nothing for them.

**Code.** `glb-parts.ts` (pure, no Babylon, no WebGL) reads a file into positions, normals, indices, colours, the hook and the size, and hands it over as the game's `Creature` (`creatureOf`); `fish-models.ts` fetches each file once per page and keeps the parsed template; `creature-kit.ts` turns it into one mesh with the shared satin material (the underwater skin takes over for a hooked fish). `creature-geometry.ts` keeps the plain types and the one shape made from code, a small pale ellipsoid (`placeholderCreature`) that stands in until a file has arrived, so a hooked fish is never missing from the line. `creature-thumb.ts` projects the triangles for the store pictures.

**Loading.** A file is fetched when it is first needed and kept for the page. `OceanScene.ready()` does not wait for any of them: once the first frame is compiled it starts `preloadFishModels`, three downloads at a time, for the ten files (6.6 MB in all, nearly five times what the old files weighed; this is the cost of keeping the models whole). A failed file is retried after 30 s and the stand-in stays; the store cell stays empty rather than showing another animal.

**Hanging.** A landed animal hangs from the rod tip by its hook (`creature-hang.ts`). Every export puts the hook on the nose axis, so every animal hangs nose up, the plain fish pose; the old exceptions for the octopus and the seahorse (hook on the head, a snout up the side) are no longer used, and how the jellyfish, the seahorse and the crab look hanging has not been seen.

**Not measured.** The models are 600 to 78,048 triangles each (the old ones about 1,000), and the store thumbnails still paint every triangle on a 2D canvas. Their cost on a phone, in the sea with several animals and in the Kho cá, has not been measured; the game has not been run with them yet.

## Map detail

(Since decision [0013](decisions/0013-bigger-sea-fewer-larger-islands.md) the sea is 3 x 3 km with eight large islands and no backdrop islands: where the paragraphs below speak of three islands, the 1.08 collision ring, a coast between 0.85 and 0.99 of the radius, hills 0.3 to 0.4 of the radius wide or the five backdrop islands, read "The big sea" at the end of this file instead.)

The islands and the sea around them carry more detail, all scenery: nothing here is solid and nothing is shared state (the boats still only collide with `islands` in `model.ts`, and the server is unchanged).

- **Islands** (`src/ocean/island-visual.ts`, `island-props.ts`, `geo.ts`): a terrain grid (76 × 76 over 1.4 of the radius; cells lying wholly deeper than 0.9 m below the waterline are dropped, the sea is opaque over them; the drawn bed dips another 0.35 m just off the sand, `drawnBed`, so a wave trough bares about 2 m of it, not 6) with patches of dry grass, ripples in the sand, a wet-sand band and a few layered rock outcrops; hundreds of grass tufts and flowers, bushes, broadleaf trees next to the palms (more palms than before), clusters of angular boulders at the shore (some in the shallows), shells and starfish, driftwood, a wooden boardwalk with rails and a lantern down to the beach, a stilted hut with a roof, a rowboat pulled 4 to 7 m up the sand, and a door, windows and a railing on the lighthouse. The palms, trees, boulders and hut built in code are now only the fallback for the Blender models (next item). The small things are written into one buffer (`Geo`) and merged with the terrain, so each island is still one mesh. Seeded: every client builds the same island. Where the sand meets the sea there is no foam mesh any more (the old flat foam ring, a transparent ground plane at 0.08 m, floated off the sand and showed through wave troughs as dark patches; it is deleted): the sea's shader draws the surf and `swash.ts` the swash, both from the real coast. A pier into open water was tried and dropped: a pier past the ring boats cannot enter (1.08 of the radius) would be sailed through, so the boardwalk ends inside the ring (at 1.06 of the radius; since the coast now lies at 0.85 to 0.99 of the radius, its last few metres stand over the shallows like a small landing).
- **Island props from Blender** (`src/ocean/island-layout.ts` places them, `island-models.ts` loads and instances them, `tests/props-models.test.mjs`). The palms, broadleaf trees, boulders, bushes and hut that were built in code are replaced by models made in Blender (`public/models/palm-1..3.glb`, about 1,000 triangles each, 5 to 8 m tall with a bent trunk and fifteen fronds; `rock-1..3.glb`, 320 triangles, faceted with moss on top; `bush-1..3.glb`, 280 to 440 triangles, low leafy domes of small lumpy clumps about 1 to 1.9 m wide, bush-2 with little flowers; `hut.glb`, 540 triangles, a stilt hut with a stepped thatch roof, a door, a window and a ladder). Each GLB is loaded once per scene (`loadMergedModel` in `model-loader.ts` merges its one-primitive-per-colour meshes into one mesh with the shared satin solid look and opaque vertex colours) and drawn as instances with their own position, yaw and scale, parented to the island mesh. Placement is pure and seeded (`layoutIsland`): **palms** 8 to 16 per island by its size (13, 16 and 11 on the three islands), most along the upper edge of the dry sand (10 to 16 m inland of the waterline, `DRY_SAND` is 11 m) and the rest up the slopes, never on ground below 0.9 m (so none stands on the open beach), on ground steeper than 0.42, within 1.6 m of a keep-out area or 3.2 m of another palm; random model, yaw and scale 0.8 to 1.25, the trunk's base sunk 0.18 m (times the scale) below the lowest ground round it. **Rocks** in clusters (a big rock, scale 1.2 to 2.2, or 0.9 to 1.4 for the widest model, with up to four smaller ones, 0.6 to 1.2) on the beaches and up the slopes, plus two to four large ones in the shallows 1 to 5 m off the sand (beach clusters are spread over all of the sand, from the waterline to `DRY_SAND − 1` m inland, so some lie in the swash); each sunk by a fifth of its scale into the lowest ground under it, and every rock stays inside the ring boats collide with (1.08 of the radius). **Bushes** (`placeBushes`, drawn last from a random stream of their own so the palms, rocks and hut stay exactly where they were): 14 to 26 per island by its size (`0.6 x radius`, so 19, 23 and 16 on the three islands), on the grass and the sand's edge only (ground at least 0.8 m high and not steeper than 0.5, nothing below the waterline under its footprint, so none on the open beach), 1.2 m from every keep-out area (boardwalk, rowboat, lighthouse, hut, market gangways, driftwood), 1.5 m from a palm, off the rocks and about a metre from other bushes; 45 % of the tries are clusters of two or three small bushes (scale 0.8 to 1.15), the rest single bigger ones (1.1 to 1.6); random model and yaw, the base sunk 0.1 x scale into the lowest ground under it. **The hut**: the most level spot 13.5 to 20 m inland of the waterline (behind the dry sand even at its front edge; preferring 15 m), its door turned out to sea (away from the island's centre); its origin is set so the four posts reach below the lowest ground under them and the ladder stands on the ground at the door, and a spot is used only if the deck then clears all the ground under the floor. The keep-out areas are the boardwalk's run, the lighthouse, the rowboat, the driftwood and the market gangways (from the pier to 1 m past where each lands, `GANGWAY_LANDING`); palms and rocks also keep off the hut. **Loading and fallback**: `createIsland` stays synchronous and still builds the procedural palms, trees, boulders, bushes and hut, now in a mesh of their own (`IslandVisual.fallback`, a child of the island mesh) from the same random stream, so the rest of the island is unchanged. `OceanScene` loads the ten models after building the islands; when all have loaded, `placeIslandProps` disposes each island's fallback and adds the instances (a second call does nothing); if any fails, a warning is logged and the procedural props stay. `OceanScene.ready()` waits for this, so the loading screen normally lifts with the models in place. Headless tests and the backdrop islands use the procedural props. Orientation: the scene is right-handed and the exporter writes Y-up glTF, so Blender's +Y (the hut's door) is the model's −Z; `yawFacing` turns −Z toward a direction (checked in renders and by the test, which finds the door's colour in the GLB). Cost: about 1,000 triangles per palm instance, so the busiest frame of the browser test rose (see "Deferred work"); the performance pass is postponed by the user's choice. Seen only in software-rendered screenshots.
- **Island shape** (`islandShape` in `island-visual.ts`, pinned by `tests/map-detail.test.mjs`). The islands used to be one bell-shaped hill 0.38 to 0.56 of the radius high with a near-circular coast, and read as fake cones. They are now low green islands in the manner of Lý Sơn or Cô Tô: two or three overlapping low hills (Gaussian, 0.3 to 0.4 of the radius wide) set along a seeded ridge, plus slow undulations, scaled so the highest point is exactly `peak` = 0.20 to 0.24 of the radius (about 6.7, 8.3 and 5.5 m on the three islands); the land is never steeper than about 0.6 (31°). The beach is long and gentle (the old one, `1.2 (1 − e^(−d/2.6))`, rose at 25° and was full height within 5 m, so the surf could not look real): with d the distance inland, the sand rises as `beachRise(d)` = 1.2 (1 − e^(−r/1.2)) with r = d (0.07 + 0.0062 d), a slope of 0.07 (4°) at the waterline that steepens slowly (0.1 at 6 m) and reaches 1 m at about 11 m (the dry sand, `DRY_SAND`), easing off at 1.2 m where the grass takes over; the hills (and the 0.9 m plateau under them) fade in from 9 m inland over the larger of 14 m and half the radius, so the hills are no steeper than before (about 0.6) and the coast did not move; below the waterline the floor is `shelfDepth(e)`, 0.07 of the distance at the sand, 0.75 m at 10 m out, 2 m at 25 m and 2.2 m at 28 m, levelling off at 8 m (`SHELF`, `BEACH`, `BEACH_TOP` in `island-shape.ts` are also written into the sea's shader). The wider sand takes about 11 m of each island's 24 to 36 m of land: the grass-and-hill core is smaller than before (the unchanged coast keeps the island's outline). The coast is not a circle: `coast(angle)` = 0.92 of the radius plus three slow harmonics (2, 3 and 5 per turn, seeded phases) and a faint crinkle, clamped to 0.85 to 0.99 of the radius, so it has bays and headlands but always stays inside the collision ring (1.08). The lighthouse stands on the highest point found by a 0.5 m search, sunk to the lowest ground under its foot. The backdrop islands use the same recipe on a coarser grid. The pure shape lives in `island-shape.ts` (re-exported by `island-visual.ts`), so the shore field, the minimap and the fishing zones use it without Babylon. Seen only in software-rendered screenshots.
- **Shore field** (`src/ocean/shore.ts`, `tests/shore.test.mjs`). At start-up each island's coast (`coast(angle)` of `islandShape`, the play area's three islands with the seeds `islandSeed(index)` the scene builds them with, then the five backdrop islands) is sampled at 512 angles into a table of radii; the distance to the coast is the radial gap to that curve divided by `√(1 + (c′/c)²)` (the curve's slant), with the outward normal from the same slant. The test checks the table against the terrain's waterline at 360 angles on every island (within 8 cm) and the distance against a brute-force polyline distance (within 10 cm near the beach, 2 % at 60 m). The GPU gets the same table as a 512 × 8 RGBA texture (16 bits per radius in R and G, read with nearest filtering and interpolated in the shader), so the water and the land cannot disagree. Also there: `seabedDepth(d)` (the island's own gently shelving sand, `shelfDepth`: 0.75 m at 10 m out and 2.15 m at 25 m, then a fall to about 11 m at 45 m out, 17 m at 60 m and the open floor at about 46 m; the GLSL twin also has `beachRise`), the surf clock (`surfPhase`, `swashFront`) and their GLSL twins (`glslShore`).
- **Surf and swash** (`water.ts` fragment shader, `src/ocean/swash.ts`). Purely visual: the water mesh is not displaced near the shore (the vertex shader still moves it by the waves and the wake only), so the boats float on exactly what is drawn. Breakers run parallel to the real beach whatever the offshore swell does (refraction): a crest reaches the sand every 6.5 s (`SURF_PERIOD`) and takes `4 √d` seconds to come in from `d` metres out, so the lines bunch up near the sand (about 2.6, 10.6, 24 and 42 m out at one moment, 8 to 18 m apart where they break). On the gentle beach the breakers are spilling ones: each crest tilts the normals with a skewed-cosine profile (long back, a gentle face, amplitude 0.3 at most instead of 0.42) that builds from 44 m out, feathers (a thin white lip with lace, stronger as it shoals) and breaks at its own distance, **9 to 24 m off the sand** (drawn per wave from slow noise, so a set breaks farther out than a lull and two or three lines are white at once; larger on the side facing the wind), and runs in as a long white bore (about 7 m behind its crest at 10 m out) whose foam thins into lace trails behind it (moving bubble cells and noise, warped, drifting toward the beach). The arrival times, break distances and strength vary along the shore (slow noise), so no line is a ring; the arrival time (`surfJitter`, shared by the sea and the swash) also has a term a few metres wide (0.3 cycle, about 2 s), so a crest breaks into separate tongues instead of one band along the whole beach. The surf zone is about 40 m wide (`nearShore` fades out at 48 to 90 m, the shoal at 44 m). Where a wave laps over the sand (a crest higher than the beach there) or a trough bares it, the shader knows the water's own depth (`column` = wave height above the bed, the bed being `beachRise` inland of the coast and the shelf off it): the water is clear over bare sand, with a thin white edge. At the waterline the spent bore is flat (no tilt) with a little white water. The beach is draped with a swash sheet (one mesh per island, 256 angles × 13 rows from 3.5 m out to 11 m inland, 7 cm above the terrain as it is drawn at every vertex, 6,144 triangles each, 18,432 for the three, up from 7,680; it fades out beyond 1.2 to 3.3 m out at sea): on the same clock the last of each breaker rushes up the sand (a quarter of the cycle, 1.75 s) to a reach that varies along the beach and from wave to wave (**3 to 9 m inland**), arriving early or late in a pattern of its own for each wave (up to 0.22 cycle either way, so successive waves do not run up in the same places) and slides back over about 4.5 s; its front is a white roll on the way up (wider the farther it runs) and leaves lace on the way back, the film behind it is thin clear water over the sand, and below the last reach the sand is darker and wet, drying as the cycle goes on (a wet band of several metres that follows the reach, not a line at the waterline). The seabed colour is seen through 1.7 times the real water path (a little murky), so the sand fades to turquoise within about 25 m. Seen in software-rendered screenshots only (still frames and 1 s sequences); not on a phone.
- **The sea** (`src/ocean/sea-layout.ts` for the data and the gull flight, `sea-props.ts` for the meshes): eight red and green channel buoys (a gate north of the harbor and a marked channel off each island) that bob and lean with the waves, seven seagulls circling the islands (instances of one small mesh), and five coarse backdrop islands beyond the edge of the sea (`SEA_BOUND`), seen through the haze and unreachable. Buoys are not solid, so a boat can sail through one.
- **Cost.** Measured in a software-rendered browser: the active triangles were about 80 000 at the harbor (every island and the backdrop in view), 40 000 to 54 000 at an island, against about 44 000 before. `tests/map-detail.test.mjs` runs headless (no GPU) and holds each island between 9 000 and 26 000 triangles and a backdrop island under 6 500. **The frame rate on a phone has not been measured**; if it is too slow, the grass, flowers and backdrop are what to cut first.
- Seen only in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390.

## Online contract and limits

Accounts are Supabase Email/Password with email confirmation and password recovery. All sea clients enter the `ocean:shared` Socket.IO channel on the existing API; user identity comes from server-validated Auth, never a payload UID. Joining validates the token once, enforces one active session per UID, validates finite bounded pose fields, bounds payload/rate and caps the sea at 100 boats. Joined sea clients alone receive pushes. Disconnect removes the matching boat; old replaced sockets cannot remove the new boat. No polling or Supabase schema writes are introduced.

### Transport (protocol 2)

`server/ocean.mjs` relays; clients still simulate their own boat. Measured with 50 simulated boats it cut one client's inbound traffic from about 350 KB/s to 6.6 KB/s while updates became five times as frequent, and moves stopped calling Supabase (about 44 calls/s before).

- **Moves are synchronous.** `ocean:move` (client to server, no acknowledgement, volatile) is an in-memory write, validated and rate limited (100 ms per socket). Too-fast or invalid moves are dropped silently; losing the lease is reported with `ocean:error`.
- **One tick, deltas only.** A fixed tick (200 ms, only when something changed) sends `ocean:delta` `{t, add:[{uid,name,x,z,heading,fishing,updatedAt}], up:[[uid,x,z,heading,fishing 0/1,movedAt]], gone:[uid]}`. A joiner first gets one full `ocean:state`. Entering and leaving are flushed at once.
- **Session checks on a timer.** The session is re-validated every 60 s per sea socket (`authRecheckMs`). A rejected token ends the connection with `ocean:error` "Hãy đăng nhập lại."; the Auth provider being unreachable never does (`authenticate` throws on 5xx, returns null only on 401/403). The client sends a renewed access token on the open connection with `ocean:token`, so a session is not cut when the first token (about one hour) expires. If a server without this handler cuts the connection, the client reconnects once with a fresh token; only a refused handshake means signing in again.
- **Playback, not chasing.** Clients keep a short history per remote boat (`src/ocean/remote.ts`) and draw it about 500 ms in the past (2.5 ticks: the sender's period, the wait for the next tick and jitter), interpolating between samples stamped with when the boat moved, not with the tick that carried them. Simulated with 60 ms of jitter and a sender whose pace drifts against the tick, a 300 ms trail left about a fifth of the frames standing still and 450 ms none (`tests/ocean.test.mjs` pins this); `ServerClock` follows the fastest recent path so jitter does not move playback. The renderer is unchanged.
- **Compatibility.** The client announces `proto: 2` when joining; the server answers with `proto`, `sendMs` and `delayMs`. A server without it keeps the old behaviour (acknowledged `ocean:command` moves, full `ocean:state` snapshots, 1 Hz, 1.3 s playback delay); a client without it keeps getting full snapshots. Deploy order does not matter.
- **Not done:** interest management (sending only nearby boats) and binary encoding; with at most 100 boats at 5 Hz they are not needed yet. The server is still one instance with in-memory state.

This Socket.IO sea is intentionally ephemeral and requires exactly **one API process/instance**: restart/redeploy clears boats and players re-enter; positions and catches are not saved. Do not scale Render to multiple instances until shared authoritative world state/broadcast is implemented. The API reads no database (it asks Supabase Auth who a token belongs to), so there is no LISTEN/NOTIFY to share the sea across instances. The adapter fails visibly and returns to the hub on connection failure instead of switching providers.

## Boat physics (Babylon.js + Havok)

See [decision 0008](decisions/0008-babylon-havok-boat-physics.md). `src/ocean/boat-physics.ts` runs the hull as a Havok rigid body and adds, as per-step impulses, buoyancy at six hull-bottom points against `src/ocean/waves.ts`, quadratic water drag that is far stronger across the keel than along it, propeller thrust, and a rudder whose authority grows with speed. Islands are static cylinder colliders (`islandCollisionRadius`, just past the visible beach), and the edge of the sea is a damped zone (`EDGE_ZONE`, 40 m before the limit, a spring with damping) with a hard backstop at the limit the server accepts. The first version was an undamped wall: a boat at full speed (12 m/s reaches the edge of the 360 m sea in about 15 s) was stopped at 26 m/s^2 and thrown back at 9 m/s, which read as a sudden stall and a slide; now it slows at about 10 m/s^2 and does not rebound. The player's boat is Havok's; the controller reads its pose each frame (clamped to the sea bounds) and publishes `x`, `z`, `heading`, `fishing` exactly as before, so the server contract did not change. Other players' boats are not simulated: they follow published poses and rest on the wave surface under them. Steering is analog, so the touch stick maps straight onto throttle and rudder; while casting the boat gets no thrust and drag brings it to rest. **Handling** (decision 0011, item 19; `src/ocean/hull-handling.ts`, pure and tested): the keel grips the water in proportion to the sideways speed (`KEEL_GRIP_RATE`, 5 per second at 3 m/s along the keel or more, on top of the quadratic drag), and the water damps the hull's turning (`YAW_WATER_DAMPING`, 2.6 per second on top of Havok's 0.9, with the rudder torque raised to 10,000 N m so the top turn rate stays about 0.6 rad/s). Held at full throttle and full rudder the course now stays within about 6 degrees of the heading (it was 21 to 27, up to 4.8 m/s across the hull), the boat keeps 88 to 94 % of its cruise speed (it lost a quarter), and after the key is let go it turns about 10 degrees more (it was 30 to 36) and has stopped turning in under a second (it was 2.8 s). The drawn boat is not the hull Havok moves: `BoatPhysics.view` holds the pose blended between the last two fixed 60 Hz steps by the time left over (`blendFraction`), because a frame is never a whole number of steps and a frame with no step followed by one with two made the heading rate and the speed judder (a turn at 0.58 rad/s measured 0.14 rad/s of spread frame to frame). The boat's visuals, the camera and `selfState()` use the blended pose; the hull's forces still read the newest step. The camera never feeds the physics: dragging the camera all the way round while a key is held changes the heading by under 0.1 rad.

The world uses right-handed axes (`enableSeaPhysics`), so heading 0 is -Z and a positive heading swings the bow toward +X, matching the movement model and the published poses. Havok steps at a fixed 60 Hz regardless of frame rate, and a frame longer than 100 ms is clamped, so a slow phone sees slow motion rather than a jump.

**Speed and parting the water** (`src/ocean/boat-physics.ts`; the wake's swell is now the simulation in `src/ocean/ripple-sim.ts`). The boat is faster: thrust 6 000 N and a top speed of about 12 m/s (it was 8; the movement model's `BOAT_SPEED` follows), about 10 m/s after three seconds. From the speed along the keel the hull now behaves like one pushing a bow wave: a hump of extra wave-making drag around 7 m/s where the bow climbs (about 5 to 8 degrees of bow-up pitch while accelerating), then from 6 to 10.5 m/s it rides up on the water (a lift at the centre, 15 % less forward drag, the bow levels out), and it leans into a turn at speed (roll about the keel from steer and speed; the other way for the other turn). A slow boat is as before. The wake is a visual of the water only, in two parts. **The swell**: each moving boat (yours and the others', whose speed comes from their played-back poses) drops a ring of wave every 0.18 s; the ring spreads at 4.2 m/s, widens and fades, and the rings along a path add up to a V whose half angle is `asin(4.2 / speed)`, narrow for a fast boat and wide for a slow one, with no angle written anywhere. The water vertex shader sums up to 64 rings (`glslWake`, the CPU twin `sampleWake`, the slope checked against a finite difference in `tests/wake.test.mjs`) and adds a low swell (12 cm at most) to the water. **The white water** (`src/ocean/wake-trail.ts` for the geometry, `wake-mesh.ts` to draw it, `tests/wake-trail.test.mjs`): each boat records its path every 0.08 s and three strips are laid on the sea along it, a camera-facing-flat ribbon each: the wash behind the stern, as wide as the hull at first and widening as it ages, and a thin arm to each side that runs back from the bow (since the Kelvin pass, see "The Kelvin wake and the bow wave" below, a straight line at 19.47 degrees whatever the speed). They follow the recorded path, so they bend in a turn. The foam is a tiling noise texture (`foamPixels`: four octaves of gradient noise, soft blobs and wisps along the path) whose coordinate along the strip is the distance travelled and across it the metres from the strip's middle, so it stays where it was made instead of sliding with the boat or stretching as the strip widens; the shader cuts the foam away where a strip is faint, so it breaks into soft patches and dissolves rather than fading like smoke, and fades with distance. (Second pass, see "The wake, second pass" below: soft cut, two taps, three vertices across, strips that end in nothing and arms that fade out in a turn.) The first version drew flat soft white discs behind the stern and took the foam from the sparse vertices of the sea grid, which looked like blobs and was rejected by the user; those are gone. It does not push the boats: they float on `waves.ts`, and the foam lies 16 cm above the waves. Seen in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390 (thin white arms from the stern at full speed, curved in a turn); the frame cost on a phone of the 64-ring sum in the vertex shader and of one small mesh per moving boat has not been measured.

**The sea surface** (`src/ocean/waves.ts`, `src/ocean/water.ts`). Twenty travelling waves drawn from a wind spectrum in three tiers (see "Layered sea, leaning crests and the sea state" and "A spectrum sea, horizontal motion and the painted swell" below: swell 40 to 70 m, medium 12 to 38 m, chop 3 to 11 m) with deep-water dispersion, headed in a band around one wind (`WIND_DIRECTION`): the long swell within about 0.3 rad of it, the short chop spread up to about 1.5 rad either side, so neighbouring waves beat into wave groups instead of a uniform corrugation, each a sharpened sine (`((1 + sin) / 2)^sharp`, normalised to a zero mean) so crests are narrow and troughs broad like a real sea. The height is still a pure vertical function of (x, z, t), identical on the CPU (buoyancy, remote boats, the fishing line) and in the vertex shader (generated from the same table), and bounded by the summed amplitudes. The slope is analytic (`waveGradient`, and `waveSlope` in GLSL, checked against a central difference in `tests/boat-physics.test.mjs`), with each wave's slope faded out with distance in proportion to its wavelength and skipped once it is zero, so the far sea only pays for the long swell. The fragment shader adds fine ripples (purely visual: they tilt the normal, never the geometry the boats float on) from a 128 × 128 tileable slope map generated at startup (`src/ocean/ripples.ts`: about 56 sines with whole-number wave vectors in a band around the wind; RG is the slope, BA the slope a quarter period later, so mixing them by cos/sin of time moves each ripple along its own direction). It is read twice per pixel, 6.7 m and 2.3 m tiles turned to either side of the wind and drifting differently (faded out by about 260 m and 110 m; mipmaps flatten it further away), and scaled by a very low-frequency value-noise field: wide calm and rough patches drifting downwind and faint streaks along the wind. The same field scales the distance roughness, so calm patches stay glassy far out and rough ones go matt. Then light through the crests, a sun glitter that widens with distance, white caps on steep crests, and near the islands the surf and the turquoise shallows of the shore field (see Map detail, "Shore field" and "Surf and swash"). Seen only in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390; **the frame cost on a phone has not been measured** (an in-browser software-render timing did not synchronise the GPU and was discarded). Counting instructions only, the new shader is about as costly as the old one where the far-sea skips apply and costlier near the camera.

**Sharpness.** The sea draws at up to 2x the CSS size. `ScaleGovernor` lowers the pixel count only after two consecutive slow windows (ignoring the first 4 s), raises it again after five fast ones, and never reacts to the 30 fps cap itself. An earlier version treated the cap as "slow" and blurred the sea permanently; `tests/ocean.test.mjs` pins that behaviour.

**Cost.** Measured in the browser test (before the map detail below): about 44k triangles and 10 meshes per frame. The Babylon chunk is 1.73 MB (409 KB gzip) and the Havok WASM 2.09 MB (658 KB gzip). Both load only when entering the sea and are excluded from the service-worker precache (`scripts/pwa-build.mjs`): the sea is online-only. Frame rate on real phones has not been measured.

`tests/boat-physics.test.mjs` drives real Havok headlessly (Babylon `NullEngine`, fixed 1/60 s steps) and checks floating on the local surface, bobbing, bounded roll and pitch, gradual acceleration and coasting, turning both ways with limited sideslip, island blocking without tunnelling, the start heading, exact repeatability and the boundary wall. Removing buoyancy, drag or the island colliders makes the matching tests fail. It runs with `tests/support/register-resolve.mjs`, which only teaches Node to resolve Babylon's extensionless deep imports. `tests/ocean-babylon-browser.mjs` runs the real scene in a browser with a fake connection: 2x draw size on a 3x screen, frame cost, labels, floating, driving and turning with one touch, and a second thumb casting while the first holds the stick.

## Hub, sign-in and loading

Three separate screens, so the game is not a web page with a game inside it.

1. **Hub** (`src/app/harbor.ts`). A sunset over drifting layered waves with the game's own fishing boat bobbing on them (`src/ui/hub-canvas.ts`, drawn on a canvas with no image file, and deliberately not a picture of the 3D map: no islands, no lighthouse; the map shows only once it is loaded), the title, one big “Ra khơi” button, a captain's card (name and the fish held on this device, or a line about the shared sea when signed out) and two round 50 px buttons (install, sign in). The hub does not show how many boats are at sea because it has no connection to know that; showing a number would need a server endpoint.
2. **Sign-in** (`src/app/account.ts`, class `account-screen`). A full-screen dialog over the same sunset (a still SVG background, not the map) with the form in a card; nothing of the game is behind it. Signing in leads straight to loading.
3. **Loading** (`startOcean`, `.ocean-loading`). An opaque full screen of the same sunset, a boat, three steps (graphics, connection, entry) and a progress bar. The map and the controls are not shown: the stick, the fishing button and the counters are hidden and only the way back stays. The screen is lifted only when the engine, the models and their shaders are ready (`OceanScene.ready()`, bounded at 8 s), the connection is up and the boat is in the sea.

Tests: `tests/harbor-browser.mjs` (touch targets and fit at 844×390, 667×375 and 390×844; no engine, model or WASM request at the hub or on the sign-in screen; the sign-in covers the screen; the loading screen hides map and controls until ready).

## Loading and connecting

Entering the sea shows the loading screen above, with three steps (graphics, connection, entry). Graphics and the network start together, and `src/platform/wake.ts` nudges a sleeping API as soon as the home screen shows, so a free Render instance (30-60 s to wake) wakes while the engine downloads. The old flow gave up after a fixed 15 s and asked the player to go back to the hub.

`src/ocean/connect.ts` retries instead: pauses of 1.5, 2.5 then 4 s, each attempt abandoned after 25 s, a total patience of 100 s, and a "the server is waking up" note after 5 s. A connection that arrives after its attempt timed out is closed rather than leaked. Errors retrying cannot fix (signed out, opened in another window, sea full, not configured) are shown at once. If graphics take over 60 s or connecting runs out of patience, the card becomes a failure message with a **Thử lại** button (reload, which re-enters the sea directly) and the HUD's **Về hub** stays reachable above it. The same card appears if the connection drops mid-game.

Tests: `tests/ocean.test.mjs` pins the retry policy; `tests/ocean-loading-browser.mjs` runs the card in a browser with a flaky, a silent and a fatal fake server. Not measured: how long a real Render cold start takes from a phone, or the real cost of the wake-up ping.

## Finding each other

Players used to start up to 40 m apart facing random directions, so the other boat was usually behind or beside the camera and nothing said where it was: two real accounts started 27.6 m apart with one 86 degrees off the other's bow, and the data was fine ("2 thuyền") while neither could see the other.

- **One harbor.** `spawn()` (`src/ocean/model.ts`) puts everyone in a small area at `HARBOR` (8 slots plus jitter), all heading 0 toward the open sea, so any two players start within about 25 m and in front of each other. The client uses it for its own start pose and announces it about 0.5 s after entering, so others see the real spot. The Render API still assigns its own join pose until it is redeployed from this code; the client's pose wins as soon as it publishes.
- **Arrows.** `src/ocean/indicator.ts` places, for any remote boat that is outside the view, an arrow on a ring inside the screen pointing toward it with their name and distance (`.ocean-boat-label[data-edge]`). Boats in view keep a plain name label, hidden beyond 75 m.
- **Expired sessions.** The server reports why a socket was refused; `AUTH_REQUIRED` now becomes "Phiên đăng nhập đã hết hạn. Hãy đăng nhập lại." (not retried) and the failure card offers **Đăng nhập lại** (sign out, back to the home screen). Before this, a revoked session was retried every 5 s for 100 s.
- **One boat per account.** A second window of the same account takes over and the first is told so; to test with two players on one device use a private window (separate storage), or two accounts on two devices.
- A server "too fast" reply to a pose update no longer ends the session.

Tests: `tests/ocean.test.mjs` (harbor and arrow maths) and `tests/ocean-multiplayer-browser.mjs` (two real clients through the real server, an arrow with distance when out of view, a revoked session, same-account takeover; only Supabase Auth is faked). Replacing the new start with the old random one, or dropping the expired-session handling, makes that test fail.

### Line seen end-on

Cast straight ahead, the camera looks along the line, so a long line is drawn short and its end looked like it stopped in mid-air. A slow ring now marks where the line enters the water while waiting, and the line's flat strip keeps one side facing the same way along its length (no twist when the line points at the camera).

### Fishing camera, fisherman, bigger islands, calmer sea

- While fishing (charging, flying, waiting, fighting) the camera moves closer and lower with a narrower view, and looks toward the end of the line (the lure, or the fish in a fight); it eases back to the chase camera afterwards. The line strip is a little wider and a small orange lure marks its end (the fish takes its place in a fight).
- The fish swims toward where it wants to be (no jumps between moods), faces the way it moves and floats on the surface. The line is nearly straight while waiting and with a tired fish (sag about 0.05–0.16 m).
- A fisherman (yellow coat, straw hat) stands at the stern holding the rod, part of the merged boat mesh. (Only the code-built fallback boat has him now; the Blender boat has no crew, see "The boat from Blender".)
- The minimap is larger (156 px; 136 px on short phones) with curved shores, shallows, palms, a lighthouse mark and a 100 m scale bar. Its shores are the real coasts (`coastFactor` reads the shore table of `shore.ts`), so the map has the same bays and headlands as the 3D islands.
- Islands are about 1.7 times bigger (radii 31, 39, 26 m); props per island are capped to keep each island under the phone triangle budget. Channel buoys moved off the larger islands.
- Wave amplitudes are about 40 % lower. The wake height is added to the water the hulls float on (`extraHeight` in boat physics), so a boat rocks on the wake of another.

### Art system, steps 2 and 3: shared materials and shapes

A SOLID material (vertex colours lit by the scene, with a look: `matte`, `satin`, `gloss`, `buoy`, `gull`), one shared per look and scene via `solidMaterial` / `useSolid`; and a GLOW material (unlit flat colour or texture, `glowMaterial`) for the fishing line, lure and rings. Water, swash, sky and wake stay as their own shaders.

The boat, the buoys, the rod holder, the lighthouse and the lure use them.
- `tests/art-palette.test.mjs` also fails when a visual file builds its own `StandardMaterial`/`PBRMaterial` or calls `MeshBuilder.CreateBox/Cylinder/Sphere/Torus` itself. Creatures (their own geometry in `creature-geometry.ts`) and the water/sky/wake shaders are not moved yet. Next: the light and shading rules, applied once in the solid and glow materials.

### Looking around the boat

Dragging a finger (or the mouse) on open sea turns the camera around the boat and tips it higher or lower (`src/ocean/orbit-camera.ts`, pure and tested; routed by the input layer's `look` option, so the stick, buttons and fishing still win where they are; the stick's corner is small on purpose, because a camera drag that starts inside the stick's zone would be taken for steering, which it was when the zone was 48% × 66%). Looking never changes where the boat goes: the thrust and the rudder follow the hull's own heading (`boat-physics.ts`), whatever the camera shows. The view stays where it is left while the boat sits still; once the boat moves (above 1.2 m/s) and no finger is down, it eases back to the default view behind the boat in about 3 seconds. Each finger (or the mouse) looking counts: the view is held until the last one lifts (`OrbitCamera.hold` counts). From the side or the front the camera looks at the boat rather than far ahead of it. Pitch is limited (-0.3 to +0.8 rad) and the camera never goes under the water.

**Chase camera framing** (`src/ocean/chase-camera.ts`, pure and tested; applied by `followWith` in `babylon-scene.ts`). The default view is 17.5 m behind the boat and 9.6 m high, looking 34 m ahead (it was 14.5 m, 6.8 m and 30 m, which showed little of the sea); while fishing it eases to 12.5 m and 6.4 m as before. Three things keep it useful: it **keeps clear of islands and piers** (`clearFraction` finds where the line from the boat to the camera first meets the shore circle or a pier plus a 2.5 m margin, the camera comes in to there, quickly, never closer than 3.5 m and lower the closer it is, and goes back out slowly; `pushOut` then moves the camera out of any circle it still drifted into, because it follows with a little lag), it **looks into a turn** (`turnLead`: the look point swings up to 0.5 rad toward the way the boat is turning and straightens when it goes straight, and fades out while fishing), and the finger look-around above works on top of it. `tests/chase-camera.test.mjs` pins the numbers, the avoidance (including 4,000 random boats on the real sea) and the lead; not yet seen on a phone.

### Fish size

A fish is drawn at its species' real size (`drawnLength` in `src/ocean/fishing.ts`: length in cm at 1 kg times the cube root of its weight, squeezed into 0.4 to 1.5 m), for the fish on the line and the one hanging from the rod. During a fight a fish far from the camera is drawn larger so it stays visible (about 4 degrees of the view, at most 3.5 times its size). It used to be 1.7 to 3.2 m for every species, an anchovy as long as a tuna.

### Art system, step 4: light and shading, once

- `src/ocean/art/lighting.ts`: the only light rig (`createSeaLights`): a warm sun from the front left and a sky fill whose underside is the blue bounce of the sea, plus the rim-light values. Objects never create lights (a test checks).
- `src/ocean/art/toon.ts`: soft cel shading (a partly applied posterize, 5 steps, amount 0.55) added by `solidMaterial` to every solid look, so hulls, hills, props and fish read as bands of colour. The solid materials also get a cool rim glow on edges that turn away from the viewer (`LIGHT.rim`). Water, sky, wake and the unlit glow materials are not touched.
- Tuning is in the constants of these two files, not in the objects.

### Proportions: line and fish against a 7.4 m boat

The fishing line is a flat strip about two pixels wide on a phone at any distance (`0.0035` m per metre of distance, at least 1.2 cm; was about 4 px, a tape). The lure is 14 cm. A fish is drawn at its real size from its species length and weight (`drawnLength`: an anchovy a hand long, a tuna as long as a person, a shark up to 4 m, never under 0.3 m); a far fish in a fight is drawn up to 3 times larger (at most 2.2 m) so it is not a speck, and shrinks to its real size as it is reeled in. A soft dark patch on the water under the hooked fish helps to find it. The hanging catch is drawn at real size (at least 0.45 m).

### Markets (selling fish)

Each island has a fish market: a floating wooden pier with a striped-awning stall, a gold-coin sign and a flag, in the shallows off the side that faces the harbor, its gangway (18.8 m from the pier's centre, `GANGWAY_REACH`, on piles over the surf) resting 8.5 m up the sand of the real coast in that direction (`GANGWAY_LANDING`: on the gentle beach the sand reaches the 0.8 m high planks only there; the pier stays where it was); the pier's solid circle still stays at least 0.5 m outside the island's collision ring (the old placement, the ring plus 9 m, left the gangway a few metres short of the sand once the coast became irregular) (`src/ocean/market.ts` positions, `src/ocean/market-visual.ts` the 3D pier; Chợ Hải Đăng, Chợ Đồi Xanh, Chợ Bãi Cọ). The minimap shows each market as a gold coin with a fish. When the boat is within 17 m of a pier (not while fishing) a glass **Bán** button appears next to the fish store button; tapping it opens the market hub (`src/app/market-ui.ts`): a full-screen list of the fish the server's ledger holds, grouped by species with what each group pays, a sell button for each group, **Bán tất cả** and the wallet in xu, with an X to close. ## Day, night and weather

The sea has a day and a weather, the same for every player and only a look ([decision 0014](decisions/0014-day-night-and-weather.md)). Nothing here reaches `waves.ts`, the boat physics, the fishing rules, the economy or the server (`tests/atmosphere.test.mjs` reads those files as text and fails if they mention the atmosphere).

**The clock.** `src/ocean/atmosphere.ts` (pure, no Babylon, tested) maps a UTC time in milliseconds to an `Atmosphere`: `atmosphereAt(utcMs)`. One game day is `DAY_MS` = 24 real minutes (`dayFraction = (utc / DAY_MS) mod 1`, 0.5 is noon, so every client agrees without a server). The weather is chosen per slot of `WEATHER_SLOT_MS` = 4 minutes by a hash of the slot number (`weatherKindOf`: clear 40 %, overcast 22 %, rain 22 %, fog 16 %, and a strength that varies from slot to slot), and `weatherAt` cross-fades each boundary over `WEATHER_FADE_MS` = 90 s (45 s each side, a smoothstep), so the weather is a continuous function of the time; the first and last 45 s of a slot are a blend, the middle is steady. Weather is four numbers (cloud cover, gloom, rain, fog), so cross-fading is just interpolating them.

**The sun.** `sunPath(dayFraction)`: a flat arc whose noon height is the sea's own sun (`SUN_DIRECTION`, 24 degrees up), sunrise and sunset at a quarter and three quarters of the day, azimuth turning with the day, the moon exactly opposite (so it is up all night, lowest at dawn and dusk). Keyframes are laid along the sun's height, not the clock (night, deep twilight, civil twilight, sunrise/sunset gold, low sun, midday), eased with smoothstep between them, so dawn and dusk are the same colours mirrored (dawn gets a rosier horizon) and nothing jumps. A game hour is a real minute: about 55 s of twilight each side, about 6 minutes of full night.

**What it drives.** One shared mutable object, `SEA` in `src/ocean/sea-atmosphere.ts`, rewritten once per frame by `stepAtmosphere()` (called at the top of `OceanScene.update`, no allocation). It holds Color3 / Vector3 objects that the shaders hold as uniforms by reference (`bindAtmosphere(material)` and `ATMOSPHERE_UNIFORMS`; Babylon's `ShaderMaterial` keeps the reference and rebinds it every frame), so the sea, the swash, the sky and the underwater skin follow without being set again. The constants of `sea-style.ts` and `LIGHT` in `art/lighting.ts` stay as the midday-clear defaults, and a test pins `atmosphereWithPin(.., {hour: 12, weather: "clear"})` to them; the render harness shows midday-clear identical to before the change (pixel difference 0 at 844 x 390). Per consumer:

- `sky-model.ts` (GLSL shared by the sky, the water and the swash) now declares `sunLight` (sun colour times strength: orange at sunset, black below the horizon, dim under cloud), `moonDirection`, `moonLight`, `nightGlow` (light added to the water and sand at night so they stay deep blue), `grade` (x exposure, raised at night like an eye adapting; y daylight; z stars) and `weather` (x cloud cover, y gloom, z rain). `sunColor()` returns `sunLight`.
- `sky.ts`: stars (a hashed grid on the sphere, twinkling, cut by cloud), a full moon opposite the sun (disc with dark seas and a soft halo, hidden under the horizon, skipped in the reflection pass like the sun), cloud coverage by the weather (cover 0 keeps the original thresholds exactly; cover 1 is a lid) and cloud shade that becomes the haze colour under a grey sky; the moon lights cloud edges.
- `water.ts`: `nightGlow` in the light that reaches the body of the water, a second GGX highlight from the moon (the glitter path, off by day), the fog density from `SEA.fogDensity`, the body colours (`deep`, `deepTeal`, `shallow`) from `SEA` (greyer and darker under cloud), and rain rings (random drops that start rings, a fragment-only normal detail like the other ripples, never the mesh). `swash.ts` gets `nightGlow` too.
- `art/lighting.ts`: `createSeaLights` also makes a moon `DirectionalLight` (intensity 0 by day) and, once per frame, copies `SEA` into the hemispheric light, the sun, the moon, `scene.fogDensity`, `scene.fogColor` and `scene.clearColor` (the fog colour is the horizon colour, so sea and sky still meet without a seam). The reflection pass needs no change: it draws the same scene with the same lights, fog and sky dome.
- `underwater-material.ts` dims the colour the hooked fish fades into at night (`SEA.murk`); the clear-water layer's sun direction is `SEA.sunDirection`.
- Fog never gets thinner than `FOG_DENSITY` (a test sweeps two days), so `water-extent.ts` (the far rings reach past the fog) still holds; rain and mist only thicken it (0.0023 overcast, 0.0040 in rain, 0.0052 in mist at full strength).

**Rain** (`src/ocean/rain.ts`): one mesh and one draw call, 1,500 camera-facing streaks (6,000 vertices, 3,000 triangles) in a 60 m x 30 m box around the camera. The streaks fall in the vertex shader (height is a function of the sea clock, the box wraps round the camera so the rain stays in the world while the camera moves), so nothing is built or allocated per frame; each streak has a rank and shows only while the rain is stronger than the rank, so rain thickens and thins smoothly; the mesh is hidden when `rain < 0.02`, is excluded from the sea's mirror (`reflection-model.ts`), and uses `SEA.rainColor` (dim at night). Drops on the water are the rings above. No sound.

**Night** (`src/ocean/beacon.ts`): the lighthouse lamp gets a camera-facing halo and two beams that sweep round the sea (period about 9 s), two additive meshes, lit from dusk to dawn (`beaconLevel`, a little dimmer in rain), skipped in the mirror. The boats' lanterns were already unlit glowing meshes and show at night as they are. Night is meant to be deep blue and readable on a phone: the HUD does not change, the water keeps a glow, the boats a blue fill light and rim, and the moon's glitter path is the main light on the sea.

**Pinning the time and weather (development and tests only).** `?daytime=<hour 0..24>` (12 is noon, 21.5 is half past nine) and `?weather=clear|overcast|rain|fog` in the page address pin that part; either one alone leaves the other on the shared clock; with neither, every player follows UTC (`parsePin`, pure and tested; `pinAtmosphere()` and `currentPin()` in `sea-atmosphere.ts` for tools). Pinned clear has no clouds at all (the original look). The render harness (`tools/render-harness/preview.html`) never follows the clock: its default is noon, clear, and it takes the same two parameters, `VIEW=844x390` sets the picture size and `EXTRA="&daytime=21&weather=rain"` adds parameters (see its README). Browser tests that look at pixels should pin the same way.

**Performance pass (new cost, nothing measured on a phone).** Sky fragment: a star-field hash and the moon (an `fbm` for the dark seas) now run per pixel at night only (both are behind a branch on `grade.z` and on the moon's direction), the cloud thresholds and shade cost a few more ALU. Water fragment: a second GGX evaluation for the moon (behind `moonLight.b > 0.002`, so off by day) and the rain rings (behind `weather.z > 0.02`: a few hashes and one exponential per pixel). Rain: 1,500 streaks in one draw call (3,000 triangles, an alpha-blended pass over the sea), only while it rains. Beacon: two draw calls, only at night. One more `DirectionalLight` on every lit material (the moon; three lights now), always on: if the shader cost matters on a phone, switch the moon off by day (`intensity = 0` still evaluates it). All of it is per-frame uniform updates of a handful of vectors and floats. The sea's mirror and the clear-water layer are unchanged in cost.

**Known limits.** Weather is the same for everyone because the clock is, but a device with a wrong clock sees the wrong hour. The moon is always full, the stars do not rotate, rain has no sound and does not wet the boats or the deck, the beams are a flat cross of strips (seen edge-on a beam is a thin bright line), overcast/rain water is greyer but the sand shelf stays turquoise, a pinned weather does not cross-fade, and lamps on other boats do not glow beyond their own unlit mesh.

## Sea light model

Sky and water share `src/ocean/sky-model.ts`: one linear-light radiance function (horizon→zenith gradient, warm glow and Mie halo toward the sun, sun disc) and one ACES tone map + gamma. The water fragment (`water.ts`) is physically based: Schlick Fresnel (F0 0.02) softened by roughness, a GGX/Smith sun specular whose roughness grows with distance, a reflection of the scene (see "Reflection pass" below; the analytic sky the dome draws is the mirror where there is no picture), Beer–Lambert absorption over the path down to the sea floor and back (`WATER_ABSORPTION` per metre, red first, then green; the depth is `seabedDepth` of the real shore distance), so over the sand shelf the water turns from deep blue through teal to turquoise and a pale sandy green at the beach, with the sandy floor visible (sand ripples, darker weed patches, seen through the refracted view) and caustics on it in the shallows (two warped layers of moving cell walls, faded below about 8 m of depth and beyond about 70 m from the camera); open water mixes a deep blue (`DEEP_WATER`) and a greener teal (`DEEP_TEAL`) in wide drifting patches, so it is not one flat tone; subsurface glow on crests against the sun and on the face of a breaker, foam lit by sun and sky, and aerial-perspective fog tinted by the sky in the view direction. Boats, markets and islands still use Babylon's Standard materials in gamma space; there are no cast shadows yet.

### Reflection pass

The islands, their palms, rocks and huts, the markets and piers, the boats, the buoys and gulls, the lighthouse and the sky are mirrored in the water. `createSeaReflection` (`src/ocean/reflection.ts`, called by `OceanScene` right after the sky is made) adds a Babylon `MirrorTexture` about the plane y = 0 (`mirrorPlane` (0, -1, 0, 0): the camera is mirrored, everything below the water is clipped), half the canvas in each direction (`REFLECTION_SCALE`, resized with the canvas and the render-scale governor), drawn every frame with mipmaps, cleared to alpha 0. Its render list is a predicate over the whole scene (`reflectsInWater` in `reflection-model.ts`), not a list, so islands, props and boats made later (other players) join it; it leaves out the water itself, the swash sheets, the wake foam and the fishing ripple rings and marker, which lie on the water. The sun disc is switched off while the picture is drawn (`Sky.showSun`): the water adds its own GGX glitter. The scene's own materials are Standard materials, so the clip plane works; the dome and the water shader are custom and need none (the dome is above the water).

In the fragment (`glslReflection` in `reflection-model.ts`) the water projects its own plane point with the camera's view-projection (the mirrored camera sees that point at the same screen position), moves it by the tilt of the reflected ray (the wave and ripple normal, so the picture wobbles with the waves; the offset is capped at `REFLECT_WOBBLE_MAX`, 4.5 % of the screen), and blurs it with nine taps on a spiral disc turned per pixel plus a mip bias; the radius grows with roughness (so with distance) and with the wobble (`reflectionBlur`). Close calm water shows a sharp island, far or choppy water a soft one. The picture holds screen colours (the scene is drawn gamma-space, the sky already tone-mapped), so `fromScreen` inverts the tone map and gamma, and the water's own tone map is applied once. `mirror = mix(skyMirror, picture, coverage)` replaces the old analytic-sky mirror in the Fresnel mix; coverage is the picture's alpha, faded at the screen edges and zero behind the camera, so the sky takes over there with no seam. The GGX glitter, caustics, surf, foam and the swash are unchanged, and the foam is mixed on top of the mirror, never reflected.

Fallback: if the texture cannot be created (`MirrorTexture` throws), a warning is logged and the water keeps the analytic-sky mirror: it always reads a transparent 1 x 1 picture until `Water.setReflection` gives it the mirror. Headless tests (`tests/reflection.test.mjs`) pin the predicate, the size, the wobble cap, the blur and the shader wiring and need no GPU. Cost: the scene is drawn twice (the mirror pass has its own render list, so the browser test's triangle count of the main pass is unchanged) plus a quarter-resolution target and nine texture reads per water pixel; it was added for looks first at the user's request and has not been measured on a phone (see "Deferred work").

### Clear water (the sea is see-through)

Decision 0011, item 18. The sea is still ONE opaque shader that writes depth (`water.ts`; its mesh, its vertex shader and `waves.ts` are untouched, so the boats float on exactly what is drawn). What changes is that what lies under the surface is now seen through it: a boat's keel and outboard leg, the market's piles, rocks in the shallows, the base of a buoy, the hooked fish and the line below the surface, each dimmed and tinted by the water between it and the eye (red goes first, then green, leaving blue-green, `WATER_ABSORPTION`) and fading out in about 2 to 4 m. Open water keeps its look: where nothing lies below, the layer is empty and the pixel is exactly what it was; the Fresnel mirror (the planar reflection, the sky) is mixed over the see-through body, so at grazing angles and at a distance the mirror still hides it, and the foam, the swash, the wake strips and the glitter lie over it.

**The design question: how to know what is under a water pixel, on a phone.** Three ways were weighed.

- (a) Draw the water alpha-blended after the scene and read a scene depth texture and a copy of the scene colour (Babylon 9.33 has `DepthRenderer` for the depth; the colour copy would mean drawing the main pass into an offscreen target and resolving it). Best looks (true thickness for everything, real refraction) but it costs one more pass over the whole scene (about 380,000 triangles, the same as the main pass), a full-canvas depth texture and a full-canvas colour copy (about 5 MB each at 1688 x 780), MSAA lost or doubled, and a blended sea that sorts against the swash sheets, the wake strips, the foam and the ripples (every one of them draws after the water at y near 0). Rejected: far too heavy for a phone and the riskiest for the sorting.
- (b) Keep the water opaque and make everything analytic: the floor already is (`shore.ts`), but a hull, the piles, the rocks and the fish would have to be painted as signed-distance ghosts for up to 12 boats in every water pixel, which is the most costly place there is, and would show blobs rather than the real models. Rejected for objects; kept for the sea floor, which is unchanged.
- (c) **Chosen, a hybrid: a small layer of the under-water things.** The few meshes that can be under the water are drawn into a render target three quarters of the canvas in each direction (4 x multisampled), each frame, with a shader of their own that keeps only what is below the waves; the water shader reads the target at its own screen position and mixes it into its body. No depth copy, no colour copy, no blending of the sea, no MSAA problem, and the objects are the real models.

**The layer** (`clear-water-pass.ts` GPU, `clear-water.ts` pure). A `RenderTargetTexture` (three quarters of the canvas in each direction, `UNDER_SCALE` 0.75: 1266 x 585 for a 1688 x 780 canvas; RGBA8 with mipmaps and a 24-bit depth buffer, no stencil, 4 x multisampled, `UNDER_SAMPLES`: about 7 MB resolved, plus the 4-sample buffers, which a tile-based phone GPU keeps in its tile memory and a desktop-style GPU keeps in memory, about 24 MB more; it was half the canvas without multisampling first and a hull's waterline was a staircase, see the limits) cleared to nothing, in `scene.customRenderTargets` like the mirror, resized with the canvas. Its list is a predicate over the scene (`underwaterCapable`, by mesh name, so other players' boats join by themselves): the boats' hulls (the Blender hulls and their instances, the code-built boat), the market piers (`market-*`, not the signs), the rocks (`model-rock-*`, `rock-*`), the buoys, the hooked fish (`hooked-fish*`) and the strip of line under the water (`gear-under-line`). It leaves out a mesh that is wholly above the highest wave (a rock on the dry beach), farther than 110 m from the camera (`CLEAR.reach`; a market pier, a 15,600-triangle mesh of which only the piles are under the water, only within 70 m, `CLEAR.marketReach`) or out of view (with a 4 m margin), so the pass costs what could be seen: 20,000 to 27,000 triangles in the harness views (6 to 26 meshes; 67,000 before the culling, with all three islands in view) and 33,509 in the busiest frame of the `ocean-babylon` test (the harbor with three boats and a market: 417,198 triangles with the layer, 383,689 without). Each qualifying mesh is dressed, the first time it is seen, with the layer's material by `setMaterialForRendering` (a per-render-pass material, so the mesh keeps its own material in the main pass and in the mirror). The shader (`UNDER_VERTEX`, `UNDER_FRAGMENT`): a fragment above the highest wave (`MAX_WAVE_HEIGHT` + 0.25 m) is thrown away before any wave is summed (almost every fragment of a boat), the rest computes the depth under the shared wave function (`waveHeight(vWorld.xz, time) - vWorld.y`, the one the sea mesh and the boats use) and keeps what is below; it lights the vertex colour flat (0.7 + 0.3 of the sun's side), takes from it `exp(-WATER_ABSORPTION x 2.4 x path)` per channel (path = 1.4 x the depth for the sunlight plus the depth along the refracted view ray, Snell's law with the sea's own 0.75) and writes `colour x sight` with `sight = exp(-0.42 x along)` as alpha (`along` is the length of the refracted view ray under the surface, never counted as shallower than a 0.3 cosine), so the water can mix it. A lit thing fades in over its first 0.3 m below the waves (`CLEAR.waterline`, `waterlineFade`), so a hull has no hard edge where it meets the water; the line is not faded. Instanced and single meshes both work.

**The water's side** (`glslUnderLayer`, `water.ts`). After the water's own body (the floor, its caustics, the scatter) and before the Fresnel mix, the glitter and the foam: `body = mix(body, layerColour x incident x 0.9, sight)`, lit with the same incident light as the sea floor (so a boat's shadow on the water darkens what is under it too). The layer is read at the pixel's own screen position moved by the slope of the waves (0.9 m per unit of slope, at most 0.7 m: what is under a ripple wobbles) and a mip bias of 0.6 (a little soft: what is under water is not crisp); the colour is divided out of the premultiplied texel and clamped, and a faint texel (sight under 0.02) is dropped so no speckle rims the shapes. Over the shallows the water is clearer: a clear sight (above 0.4; between 0.1 and 0.4 it is blended in; a faint one, the rim of a shape, is left alone so no outline is raised around shapes) is raised to a power that goes from 0.5 (shelf, 3 m of water or less) to 1 (13 m or more) with the same smoothstep the water's own colour uses (the first version cut the sight off below 0.004 and jumped to 0.06 there; it is continuous now, pinned by a test that walks the sight in steps of 0.0005 at five depths of water), so there is no jump where the shallows meet the deep (pinned by a test: the largest change in sight per 5 cm of water depth is under 0.02). The test `underOn` is one uniform: off, there is no texture read and the sea is the one it was.

**The hooked fish and the line.** With the layer on (`FishingGear.setClearWater`) the fish wears the ordinary shared satin skin: the opaque water hides it by depth, the layer draws it with the rest (so a swell between the camera and the fish no longer hides it, and the special depth trick is not needed), and the strip of line under the water is drawn by the layer only (the main pass writes neither colour nor depth for it), with its own flat material that takes the line's tension colour and is drawn as wide as the line above the water (`CLEAR.lineWiden` is 1; at half the canvas it had to be 2.4 times wider or it broke into dashes, at three quarters with multisampling it does not). Off, the old special `underwater.ts` skin is used exactly as before (decision 0011, item 17); the switch can happen at any frame (`wear` picks the skin each frame). Both are tested (`tests/underwater.test.mjs` is unchanged and runs the old path; `tests/clear-water.test.mjs` the new one and the switch).

**The switch** (`ClearWaterGovernor` in `clear-water.ts`, driven by `OceanScene.adapt`). It starts on (if the render target could be made and the URL has no `?clearwater=0`), goes off after two slow windows in a row (2 s each, average above 52 ms, the render scale's own bar, after 4 s of warm-up), comes back after eight fast windows (16 s, below 40 ms) and at most twice, then stays off. While it is on the render scale is held (`ScaleGovernor.sample(dt, hold)`): the see-through sea is given up first (it is the newest cost and the look is fine without it), and only then does the resolution coarsen. `OceanScene.setClearWater(on, pin)` forces it (`pin` holds the state against the governor: the browser test uses it, so slow software frames cannot switch the layer off in the middle of a check) and `stats()` reports `clearWater` and `clearWaterMeshes` (how many meshes the layer drew last frame). Forcing the state also starts a new measuring window (`ClearWaterGovernor.set`; before, a window half measured under the old state could switch it off a moment after it was switched on; pinned by a test that fails without the reset). Off is the old opaque sea, nothing else changes.

**Limits and visual downsides.** **What a review found and how it was fixed:** at half the canvas, even with a mip bias of 0.9, the player's own keel under the hull came out as a saw-tooth red line or three or four detached red blocks (the foam collar covers the rest). Fixed with three things together: the layer is multisampled (4 x) so a silhouette has a coverage ramp, not stairs; it is three quarters of the canvas (0.5 first); and a lit thing fades in over its first 0.3 m below the waves; a bias of 1.6 also hid the stairs but made the fish and the line a pale smudge, so the bias stays small (0.6). The keel now is a soft dark red-brown shade under the hull, partly hidden by the foam. Costs of the fix: the target went from about 3.1 MB to about 7 MB (plus the multisample buffers) and it has 2.25 times the pixels; multisampling is an extra cost on a GPU without tile memory, and a device that cannot multisample just gets stairs (Babylon clamps the count). The fish and the line under the water are softer and paler than the old special skin drew them (the fish keeps its stripes at close range, the line is a pale yellow ribbon, a little wider than above the water), the price of the see-through look. The sea's edge: the layer fades out over the last 3 % of the screen at its borders (as the reflection does), so a buoy or a keel right at the edge of the screen fades with it. A boat's draft is only 0.6 m (the outboard leg goes down 1.9 m), and the foam collar and the wake lie over the waterline, so from the chase camera the keel is a hint of dark red-brown under the hull, not a clear hull: the hull of this boat is mostly above the sea. There is no refraction of position (only the wobble) and the thickness is the object's depth under the wave at its own x, z (not along the real ray), a few centimetres off where the wake lifts the water. The wake height (up to 12 cm) is not in the layer's depth. Objects under the sea floor of an island (the buried foot of a rock) show through it; a sandbar does not hide a hull behind it (the floor is analytic, not geometry). One more shader to compile (the layer's, two variants for single and instanced meshes), done in `ready()` (`ClearWater.compile`). Seen in software-rendered images only (a temporary copy of the render harness page that also loads the Blender boat and the fishing gear, since deleted; 844 x 390, 932 x 430 and 667 x 375; chase and fishing cameras; fish surge, strong and tired; a four-frame sequence); not tried on a phone. The committed harness page (`preview.html`) does not create the layer: it shows the opaque sea.

**Cost**, all new (also under "Performance pass"): one more pass per frame over the under-water meshes (about 20,000 to 27,000 triangles in the harness views; 33,509 in the `ocean-babylon` browser test's busiest frame: 417,198 triangles with the layer, 383,689 without), one render target (three quarters of the canvas, 4 x multisampled: about 7 MB resolved for a 2x phone, plus the multisample buffers), a mip chain generated each frame (a third of a layer), a fragment shader that touches only the pixels below the waves (a handful of `sin`/`pow` for the wave height on the hulls' and piles' lower parts), and in the water shader one matrix product, one texture read with a bias and one `pow` for each water pixel (the `pow` and the mix only where the layer has something). Nothing of this has been measured on a phone.

### The boat from Blender

The player's boat, and every other player's, is `public/models/boat.glb` (built by `tools/blender/boat.py`, 3,697 triangles: hull 1,479, deck 964, canopy 680, engine 362, lantern 144, flag 68): a round wooden boat with a sheer rising to a raked bow, a cream boot stripe and a rubbing strip, a wheelhouse forward of midship under a domed roof (front window, portholes, a door, a life ring), a mast with a pennant and a lantern, a cute outboard on the transom, bow lights, a rod holder at the starboard stern (where `ROD_BASE` is), a fish crate and a rope coil on an open stern deck. It has no fisherman. The root `boat` is at the waterline, midship, with the six parts as children (each at its own pivot); the physics box (2.8 × 1.4 × 7.4 m) is unchanged. The hull is 3 m wide with the rubbing strip and the outboard hangs 0.6 to 0.7 m behind the box; the boat collides with the invisible cylinders of the islands (1.08 of the radius, beyond the sand) and of the piers, so reversing into one can dip the outboard up to 0.7 m into a cylinder that stands in open water, where there is nothing to see: it does not matter.

- **Loading** (`src/ocean/boat-model.ts`, `boat-paint.ts`, the same pattern as the island props): `OceanScene` builds every boat from code at once (`createBoatTemplate`, the old boat with the fisherman) and loads the GLB in the background; when it arrives every boat drawn so far (and those that join later) swaps its hull for instances of the model, and `ready()` waits for it. A failed load logs a warning and the code-built boat stays. A boat is now a `TransformNode` (the pose node the physics or the sea places) whose children are the hull instances, the rod and the line.
- **Axes.** The file's bow is at glTF +Z with the rod holder at x = −1.25, z = −2.52 (boat.py turns the copy it exports half a turn about Z). The scene is right-handed, so the loader adds no flip: the bow would point to +Z. `BOAT_YAW` (π) is baked into the merged vertices (x → −x, z → −z, a pure turn, no faces flip), which puts the bow at −Z and the holder at (+1.25, 2.52). Checked in renders from the chase camera (the cabin is forward of the rod, the outboard at the stern) and by `tests/boat-model.test.mjs` (the bow end, the green light starboard and forward, the holder's collar within 0.2 m of `ROD_BASE`).
- **Colours per player.** The file carries the first palette's two roles as whole bytes: the hull paint 0x2A6FB8 (the hull and the wheelhouse's dome) and the trim 0xFFD35A (rubbing strip, roof rim, the engine's cowl band, the pennant). `paintBoat` replaces every vertex of exactly those colours with the player's `boatPalettes` colours and keeps all others; one painted mesh per palette is built once and every boat is an instance of its palette's mesh (hull plus lantern = two draw calls, plus the rod's two).
- **The lantern** is its own small mesh with the unlit glow material, so it glows in the shade (every other part is the shared satin solid look).
- The boat's meshes are in the reflection pass like every other mesh (name predicate).

### Boat shadows on the water

`water.ts` darkens the sea in the shadow of every boat (yours first, then the fleet, up to `BOAT_MAX` = 12). There is no shadow map: each boat is three parts (hull, wheelhouse, mast) swept through their heights (4, 4 and 10 samples; a single slice per part left the wheelhouse and mast shadows lying apart from the boat as a detached dark patch), each footprint shifted along the sun's ground direction by `height / tan(elevation)`, so the low sun throws a long shadow away from it that stays in one piece with the hull (`SUN_DIRECTION` in `sea-style.ts`). In shadow the water loses 62 % of the sun's light (body, glitter, foam) and 20 % of the sky light and mirrored sky (`SHADOW` in `src/ocean/boat-shadow.ts`; it took 90 % and 45 % first, which on the deep sunset water made a hard black slab, and `shadowKeeps` is tested to leave the water about half its light), and the edge of each slice softens with the height it is thrown from (a real penumbra). Boats do not shadow islands or each other's decks, and islands/markets cast no shadow on the sea.

## Market vendors: the three cats

Each fish market has a vendor (`vendor` on `Market` in `market.ts`: a name, a greeting and `look`, one of `lan`, `tu`, `sau`): Cô Lan (Hải Đăng, `lan`, a slim cream calico), Chú Tư (Đồi Xanh, `tu`, the big ginger tabby) and Bà Sáu (Bãi Cọ, `sau`, the small round grey elder). They are chibi cats dressed like people, made in Blender (`tools/blender/vendors.py`) and committed as `public/models/cat-lan.glb`, `cat-tu.glb` and `cat-sau.glb`. They stand on the pier deck in front of the counter, facing the sea, and are decoration and flavour only: no gameplay effect, no server state, no chat. When the market hub opens, the vendor's name and greeting show under the title (`app/market-ui.ts`, unchanged).

**What loads.** Each file is one root `vendor-<id>` at the feet with exactly five children: `body`, `head`, `arm-left`, `arm-right` and `tail`, with no rotation or scale on any node. The pivots are the head at the neck, the arms at the shoulders and the tail at its base, so every part can be rotated about its own origin. The cat's right arm is at glTF -x; the model faces +Z, and the market turns it half round to face the sea (checked in a render). The files have vertex colours (`COLOR_0`) only: no textures, no skeleton. `loadPartsModel` in `model-loader.ts` loads a GLB and keeps each named part as ONE merged mesh (the exporter writes one primitive per palette colour) with the sea's shared satin solid look, hung from a pivot node placed where the part's own node was. `cats.ts` loads the three (`loadCats`, all or none, once per scene) and `market-vendor.ts` (`placeVendors`) parents each cat to its market's pier. `createMarket` stays synchronous and the pier is complete without a cat: while the models load, or if a load fails (a warning is logged), the stall simply has no vendor. `OceanScene.ready()` waits for the cats as it does for the island props. The cats are drawn at `CAT_SCALE` (1.6) times their file size (1.2 to 1.33 m), larger than life, as the old vendors were, so the face and the wave read from a boat.

**Animation** (`cat-pose.ts`, plain maths, no Babylon): from the sea clock (`OceanScene` passes its own clock, so a reduced-motion player gets a still pose), no skeletal rig, only rotations of the five nodes. The cat breathes slowly (the body's height scales by up to 0.8 % and the head, arms and tail rise with it, a few millimetres), the head tilts a little (0.07 rad) and nods (0.05 rad), the tail sways about its base with a swing, a tempo and a phase of its own per cat (`CAT_MOTION`), and the right arm waves for 2.2 s in every 8 (`waveAmount`, as the old vendors did). The market's phase (`market.x * 0.37`) keeps the three out of step. Per frame it writes about a dozen numbers; it adds no draw call beyond the five meshes of each cat.

**Cost and limits.** The three cats are 3,709 (Lan), 3,475 (Tu) and 3,251 (Sau) triangles, 10,435 together in 15 meshes (five per cat); the old code-built figures were about 3,500 each. The parts are rigid (no smooth bending of an arm), and a cat can stand, breathe and wave but not walk or sit. Nothing was run on a real phone and no frame rate was measured. `tests/cats.test.mjs` pins the GLB node names and pivots, no rotation or scale, vertex colours, the triangle budget (2,500 to 4,000 per cat), the height (1.1 to 1.4 m) and the lowest point at 0, the three cats' names and looks, the poses (deterministic, bounded, the three cats distinct, the 2.2 s of 8 wave on the right arm only); `tests/market.test.mjs` checks every market has a cat.

The five people built in Blender (`public/models/people-1..5.glb`, `tools/blender/people.py`, see "Blender pipeline" below) are not used by the game; they stay in the repository and are still checked by `tests/models.test.mjs`.

**Taller sea and water at the hulls** (`waves.ts` `SWELL_GAIN`, `water.ts` `hullWater`; user feedback: "the water looks flat, like a solid block, and the boat does not touch it"). The waves are raised from the base table (`BASE_WAVES`, 0.8 m summed) to about 1.4 m: the long swell 1.85 x, the short chop 1.35 x (gain by wavelength, smoothstep from 10 to 40 m), so the sea is taller without getting spiky; the CPU and the shader still share one table. Three knock-ons: the white caps and the thin-crest glow divide their thresholds by the gain (`SEA_GAIN`, else every crest turns white), the drawn sea floor just off the sand dips `BED_DIP` 0.65 m (was 0.35) and the shader's shallow-water maths use that same floor (else troughs painted a milky sheet along the beach), and `tests/boat-physics.test.mjs` pins the new height. **At each hull**, moving or not (the wake only exists above 1.5 m/s): a collar of ragged white water lapping at the waterline, wider and whiter toward the bow, and rings of ripple that spread from the hull about a metre a second in slow pulses with a few crests of foam, up to about 8 m out. The rings are not perfect ellipses (user feedback: concentric white rings looked fake): the distance they are measured from is warped by two octaves of slow noise, so neighbouring rings drift apart, and their foam is cut into patches by noise and stops about 3.4 m out (it was 5), so no closed white U-shaped bands trail the stern. Purely visual (the water is tilted and painted near each hull, the mesh is untouched), for the 12 nearest boats of the `boats` uniform. Seen only in software-rendered images, never on a phone. Known cost: the heavier fragment shader makes `tests/ocean-fishing-browser.mjs` (timing-sensitive under SwiftShader) fail about half the runs where it passed 3 of 3 before; with `hullWater` disabled it passes again. Not a phone measurement, but part of the performance pass. The render harness has `hull` and `openhull` views and a boat speed (`shoot.mjs ... openhull,0,0,6,10`).

**Layered sea, leaning crests and the sea state** (decision 0011, item 20; user: "make the sea like Sea of Thieves", step 4; `waves.ts`, `water.ts`, `boat-physics.ts`; tests `waves`, `boat-physics`). `waves.ts` is now eleven waves in three tiers that read as separate layers: a SWELL (56, 71 and 92 m, slow, tall, close to the wind), a MEDIUM tier (14.5, 20, 28 and 38 m: the one a 7.4 m hull pitches and rolls on) and a CHOP (3.1 to 10 m). `SWELL_GAIN` and `BASE_WAVES` are gone: the table holds the amplitudes of the roughest sea (summing to 2.4 m). **Leaning crests** (`lean` on the swell and the 38 m and 28 m waves, 0.25 to 0.35): the phase is bent, `theta - lean sin(theta)`, so the travel-side face is steeper than the back like a Gerstner crest, but the surface is still a pure vertical function of (x, z, t) (no horizontal displacement, so the CPU height at a point is the height the mesh shows there); the bend is strictly increasing for `lean < 1`, so a crest never folds (tested), and the slope is analytic (with the factor `1 - lean cos(theta)`). **The sea state**: `seaState(t)` in 0..1, two sines (330 s and 137 s, weights 0.65 and 0.35, so it never leaves 0..1), starting at 0.36 at t = 4 s (a fishing sea) and reaching about 0.96 at t = 451 s and about 0 at t = 255 s. Each tier's amplitude is scaled by `CALM_GAIN + (1 - CALM_GAIN) * state` (swell 0.58, medium 0.55, chop 0.7 at calm), so the calmest sea sums to 1.39 m (the sea before this change was 1.4 m) and the roughest to 2.4 m; `waveHeight`, `waveGradient` and the GLSL (`seaState`, `tierGains`) are the same function of the same sea clock, and a test checks the shader text against the table and the constants. **Knock-ons.** The white caps and the thin-crest glow divide by `SEA_GAIN` (the calm sum over 0.825, times 1.2), so a rougher sea gets more caps; `MAX_WAVE_HEIGHT` (2.4 m) feeds the clear-water layer's top. The shore paint (`column` in `water.ts`) reads only `SHORE_WAVE` 0.4 of the wave height at the waterline and fades to the full height 34 m out, so the tall swell does not flood the beach (a first image showed the waterline pushed many metres up the sand at a crest); the mesh and the boats are unaffected. Swash (`swash.ts`) and the shore field (`shore.ts`) do not read the wave height. **The hull** (`boat-physics.ts`): the water's damping of the turn now acts through the deep keel (`keel`, not `wet`), as the sideways grip already did: with the taller medium waves lifting the hull, the damping fell with `wet` and a held turn ran on 14.8 degrees after the key was let go (the old sea: 9.7); with the keel it is 8.5, and `tests/boat-physics.test.mjs` is unchanged in what it demands. **Cost** (also under "Performance pass"): 11 waves against 12, but the 5 leaning waves cost one more `sin` in the height and, in the slope, a `sin` and a `cos`: the height sum is 16 `sin` against 12, the slope 16 `sin` and 16 `cos` against 12 and 12, plus two `sin` for the sea state in each of `waveHeight` and `waveSlope` (every pixel and vertex), the slope's far-distance skips unchanged. Seen only in software-rendered images (a rough sea at t = 451 s, a calm one at t = 255 s, the start at t = 4 s); not on a phone.

**A spectrum sea, horizontal motion and the painted swell** (decision 0011, item 22; user: make the shape of the open-sea waves read from the chase and low cameras, then sharp crests; `waves.ts`, `swell-look.ts`, `water.ts`, `water-extent.ts`, `clear-water.ts`, `underwater.ts`; tests `swell-look`, `waves`, `boat-physics`, `shore`, `clear-water`, `underwater`). The beach, surf, wake, bow wave and islands are not changed. **The wave set** is drawn, not hand-picked: `drawWaves()` (seeded, the same sea on every device) makes 20 waves from a wind spectrum: wavelengths run geometrically from 92 m to 3 m (2 % jitter), amplitude is Phillips-like (proportional to the wavelength) with a JONSWAP bump at 58 m and a 0.7 to 1.3 spread, each tier is scaled to a fixed total (`TIER_TOTALS`: swell 1.41 m, medium 0.87 m, chop 0.125 m), so the calm height (1.38 m) and the roughest (2.405 m, under 2.6 m) and the boats' handling are as before. Headings spread around `WIND_DIRECTION`, wider the shorter the wave, alternating side from wave to wave, so no neighbours run alike; sharpness 2.0 to 3.6; waves of 20 m and more lean. **Horizontal motion, now followed by the physics.** Each wave moves every point of the sea along its heading by `(q / k) cos(psi)` (Gerstner), so points before a crest move toward it: crests pinch to a point, troughs widen. The vertex shader draws the vertex that starts at a rest position `p0` at `p0 + fade * D(p0)` with the height `restHeight(p0)` (the old vertical sum). **`waveHeight` (boats, buoyancy, remote boats, the fishing line, the wake) now returns the height of the vertex that lands at the query point**: `restPoint` solves `p0 + fade * D(p0) = (x, z)` with `REST_STEPS` = 4 Newton steps (the Jacobian `I - fade * M + D x grad fade` is summed in the same loop as the motion; a step is one pass over the 20 waves), and `waveGradient` is the rest slope through the inverse transpose of that Jacobian. `restHeight` and `restGradient` keep the old vertical function (the vertex shader's height, the shore). The shaders that read the surface at a world point under the water (`clear-water.ts`, `underwater.ts`) use the GLSL twin `seaHeight` (same Newton steps, same island fade as constants). **Measured** (`drawnSurfaceError` and `restPoint(...).residual`, 6,000 points over 3 km at t = 255, 380, 451 and 452.7 s): at the calmest sea the drawn surface and the physics agree to under 1e-6 m; at the roughest, worst error under 1 mm and Newton residual under 0.3 mm. `HORIZONTAL_LIMIT` (0.03 m) and a residual of 0.05 m are tested. **Pinch**: `PINCH` swell 0.3, medium 0.55, chop 0.3 (per tier, shared out by amplitude); the smallest Jacobian determinant of the mesh over the same points is 0.61 at the calmest sea and 0.25 at the roughest (tested: above 0.12, below 0.5), the largest horizontal motion 5.4 m, so the mesh never folds (a try with 0.4 / 0.7 / 0.3 reached 0.06 and the Newton residual grew to 0.2 m, so it was dropped). Measured on a profile along the wind at the roughest sea, a crest's curvature at its top is about 1.75 times that of the unmoved sea, but the width at 70 % of its height is nearly the same (10.5 m against 10.25 m) and the steepest slope of the drawn surface is only about 15 degrees. That is pointed, not a spike: height is held to 2.6 m, and the 40 to 92 m waves that carry it cannot be much steeper without a taller sea (a shorter spectrum, longest 72 m, was tried and made the heading rate of a turning boat too uneven for `boat-physics.test.mjs`, so it was dropped). The motion fades to zero within 70 m of an island's mean coast and is full at 170 m (`horizontalFade`, from the island list only, as constants in the shaders: the vertex shader knows no surf or coast field; `shore.test.mjs` allows exactly that), so the water at a beach is the exact analytic surface. The fragment shader reads the slope at the vertex's rest point (`vOrigin`) and corrects it with `(I - M)^-1` summed in the same loop (no extra `sin`). **The painted swell** (`swell-look.ts`, open sea only): `waveSlopeSplit` returns the swell and medium slope apart from the chop; the normal takes the swell's slope 4 times over, 40 % of the chop and 30 % of the ripple map; the body is lit by the sun on each face, a trough is darker (0.5) and a crest paler (1.12) by its height over `waveReach(t)`. **Crest foam**: streaks where height plus steepness pass a threshold, broken by noise stretched along the crest and a finer lace, switched on by the sea state only (`crestFoamAmount`: none below 0.55, full above 0.92, so none in the calm sea), fading 180 to 420 m. **The curl look** (`CURL`, a painted lip, no geometry: a height-field mesh cannot overhang without folding, and the mesh is never folded): on the tallest crests of a rough sea the front face (slope along the wind) is darkened as if undercut, the crest line (slope along the wind near zero) gets a thin bright edge, and foam runs down the front face as streaks along the wind. Honest result: in the images it reads as a breaking crest line, a bright foam edge over a dark face, not as an overhanging curl. **Mesh density**: 88 rings (`NEAR_RINGS` 80, was 56, so about 2.9 m between rings at 30 m and 6.2 m at 120 m) and 192 slices (was 96): 33,600 triangles in the sea (was 12,288), about 2.7 times as many. A first try with 118 rings and 288 slices (67,680 triangles) made `ocean-babylon` fail twice under software WebGL (the frames were so slow that the fake remote boats' labels were gone), where 88 and 192 pass, so the density stops there; more would show on a real GPU only after measuring. No facets or shimmer were seen on the crests at the chase, low and high cameras (the images were shot at the larger 118 x 288 mesh and not re-shot at 88 x 192 for every view). Seen in software-rendered images only (chase and low cameras at t = 255, 380, 451; the high view; four frames 451 to 454, where foam travels along the crests with them), not on a phone. **Performance pass** (cost added, nothing measured on a phone): the physics `waveHeight` costs about 5 passes over 20 waves (4 Newton steps plus the height; before 1) and it is called for every boat, line and wake sample (about 83 times a frame for the wake of a moving boat); the vertex shader about doubles its wave work (height as before, plus the motion: 20 `cos` and 9 `sin`, plus the island fade of 8 islands) and has 2.7 times more vertices; the fragment shader's slope loop is the same 20 waves with a few more multiplies, one `waveReach`, three more noise reads where crest foam is drawn (rough sea, open water, within 420 m) and the island fade; the clear-water and under-water shaders now solve `seaHeight` (4 Newton steps of 20 waves) per pixel below the waves instead of one `waveHeight`; no new texture, no new pass.

**A steeper sea** (user: crests still not as sharp as Sea of Thieves). The spectrum is shorter: `SPECTRUM.longest` 70 m (was 92), `peak` 44 m (was 58), swell from 40 m (was 50); the tier totals are unchanged, so the same height now sits on shorter waves and the slopes rise (measured over 600 m x 600 m at the roughest sea: mean slope 3.2 to 3.8 degrees, steepest about 25 to 26.5 degrees, which is mostly chop). `PINCH` swell 0.3 to 0.45 and medium 0.55 to 0.6 (the mesh still passes the Jacobian test). Tried and dropped: longest 62 / peak 38 / swell 34 made an idle boat roll 13 degrees in the idle-boat test (limit 12), so a fishing boat would rock too much. Checked by unit tests only (no browser run, no images, nothing on a phone).

**A clearer sea floor** (user: make the water see-through like Sea of Thieves; `water.ts`, paint only). `seeDepth` is how deep the floor looks: a wide shelving shallows to about 320 m off every coast and deep blue beyond about 950 m (`lookDepth`, at most about 33 m, so the deep floor stays faintly visible: murk factor 0.8, dunes and hollows of the floor), scaled 0.4 to 1.3 by two noise octaves, so sandbanks (shallow, pale) and channels (deep, blue) wander through it; physics and island floors keep `seabedDepth`. **Shoals** (user: small fish schools swimming round the islands, for a livelier sea; `shoals` in `water.ts`, paint only). The floor is cut into 14 m cells, four in five hold a school of 7 fish that circle a point which itself orbits the cell's middle (about 1 m/s, so a school never leaves its cell and one cell is read per pixel); each fish is a dark ellipse (1.7 m by 0.6 m) along its heading with a wiggling tail, darkening the floor by up to 80 %, softer with depth. Applied only over the shallows (6 to 18 m off the coast, look depth 0.3 to 17 m, in sight to about 170 m). Seen in software-rendered images only (a first version used a 1 m cell by mistake and showed nothing); phone cost unmeasured (7 hashes and ellipse tests per shallow pixel).

**The wake, second pass** (user feedback with a phone screenshot: a blocky white wash behind the stern made of square cells with stair-stepped edges, cut off in a hard straight line, thin white outlines forming closed loops round the boat, a black hard shadow, thin parallel lines at the end of the wash; `hull-water.ts`, `boat-shadow.ts`, `water.ts`, `wake-trail.ts`, `wake-mesh.ts`; tests `hull-water`, `wake-trail`). Root causes, found with a synthetic harness page (a scripted boat, the wake recorded at 10 to 60 frames a second) and with the hull water switched off and on: **the "wash" with cells and the hard cut were not the wake strips but `hullWater` in the water shader.** Its collar and rings were measured in the units of an ellipse (`(r - 1) * 1.2`, r = the point's distance in half-lengths), which made one edge unit about 1.2 m across the beam but 3.2 m along the keel: the collar a few metres wide became a tail 7 m behind the stern, and the rings drifted along the hull as long closed loops. The collar's width came from a single lattice of value noise at 0.3 m (`noise(p * 3.4)`), whose plateaus and straight steps, thresholded, are square cells about 10 to 15 px at a phone's size; and the whole effect was skipped beyond 10 m (`dot(d, d) > 100`) while the ripples and the collar still reached 13 m and more, so everything was cut off along a circle round the boat (the "straight line", seen across the white water and the rings alike). The closed outlines are the same rings (commit 36ed29d had already broken them up; the screenshot probably predates it, which could not be checked). Fixes: the collar and the rings are measured in metres from the waterline (`hullDistance` in `hull-water.ts`, a rounded box, the same along the keel as across it; a first try, an ellipse's level set over its gradient, left a band of "near" water along the axis ahead of the thin bow and was rejected in the first images), their slope fades to nothing by 6.5 m from the hull and the foam by 4 m, the shader's reach is 12 m (`HULL_REACH`, tested to lie outside everything that is drawn, so nothing is cut), and the collar and the finest crest patches use `softNoise` (two lattices turned against each other, contrast restored). **The strips** had their own faults. (1) The foam texture was value noise through a clamped contrast (a fifth of its pixels pure black or white, steps of 168 levels between neighbours) and was stretched across the strip (u 0 to 1 whatever the width, so an arm 0.4 m wide held the whole texture: the thin parallel lines); it is now four octaves of gradient noise through `tanh` (no plateau, no step above 48 levels) and u is in metres. (2) A trail that began less than `TRAIL_LIFE` ago ended at its first point with the opacity that point had (a boat that set off at speed left a strip with a straight end: 0.96 in the old code), and the strips have 3 vertices across so the sheet follows the waves; the first points now taper in over `START_TAPER` 0.5 s, so every strip ends in nothing. (3) In a turn the inner arm sweeps through the middle of the turn and the outer one draws a loop, and the wash folded over itself where it was wider than the turn: the arms fade out with the heading change since they were laid (`armKeep`: gone after about a radian, or at a radian a second of yaw) and the wash is held to 0.7 of the turn radius. The shader uses two taps of the texture (turned against each other), a soft cut (a patchy coverage, not an outline), soft ragged sides, thin foam showing the water through it, and the arms are broad (0.4 m wide at the bow, 0.45 m wider a second) and fainter (0.6). The 64 rings of `wake.ts` are not changed: their swell is at most 6.5 cm, under the 16 cm the foam lies above the waves. Seen in software-rendered images (844 x 390 and larger, 10 to 60 frames a second, straight, turning, stopped), not on a phone. **The collar, thinned** (user: "the foam halo is far too thick and big"): the first collar reached `(0.8 + 1.4 * lap) * push` edge units, up to about 5 m from the hull, 1.9 times wider ahead of the bow, so a round bubbly cloak wrapped the whole boat and ran ahead of the stem; it is now `COLLAR` in `hull-water.ts` (`collarWidth`, `collarFoam`, `glslCollarWidth`, the GLSL generated from the same numbers): a lap 0.5 to 0.9 m from the waterline along the sides (the planking flares about 0.35 m over the waterline, so about half a metre shows from above), 1.4 times wider over the forward 1.8 m (a small splash at the stem that is gone 0.2 m past the tip, so nothing lies ahead of the bow) and 1.3 times wider at the stern corners and gone 0.15 m behind the transom (a first try ran 0.7 m behind it and drew a U of foam round a stopped boat's stern; the wake strips take the white water on from the transom), cut into pieces by a slow noise (`gaps`, a smooth step, no hard edge), never more than 1.3 m from the waterline anywhere. The ring-crest foam is cut to 0.35 of its strength and `FOAM_REACH` 1.3 edge units (1.6 m); the rings' ripples are unchanged. The gap noise is one more noise read per pixel within 12 m of a hull (listed with the wake pass under "Performance pass"; not measured on a phone). The shader does not know the boat's speed, so a stopped boat has the same thin collar; the wash at speed is the wake strips'. Pinned in `tests/hull-water.test.mjs`.

**The Kelvin wake and the bow wave** (decision 0011, item 20; `wake-trail.ts`, `bow-wave.ts`, `bow-wave-mesh.ts`, `wake-mesh.ts`; tests `wake-trail`, `bow-wave`). Visual only: `waves.ts`, the water vertex shader, the boat physics (and its bow-wave drag) and the clear-water layer are untouched. **Arms**: each arm point is laid at the stem (`BOW` 3.7 m ahead of the boat's position) on the heading the boat had then and sits `ARM_ROOT + ARM_SLOPE x (metres sailed since)` to the side, `ARM_SLOPE = tan(asin(1/3))`, so behind a boat that holds its course the arms are a straight V of half angle 19.47 degrees at every speed (they used to spread at the wave speed, which made the angle depend on speed and the arms thin lines). The arm is a band that thickens as the foam piles up (half width `0.3 + 0.075 x sailed` m), stays strong longer (`armAlpha` fades from 1.0 s), shows from 4.5 m/s, and still fades out in a hard turn (`armKeep`). **Core**: the wash behind the stern keeps its base width and fade, and its width and opacity vary along the path with `coreWidth` (about 0.75 to 1.25 x) and `coreDensity` (about 0.7 to 1.0 x), pure functions of the distance travelled, so the core swells, pinches and thins in patches fixed to the water, and dissolves with its age as before. **Bow wave** (`bow-wave.ts`): from 1.5 m/s a crest of white water, a 17 x 3 vertex sheet lifted up to 0.9 m off the sea (`CREST_HEIGHT`, tallest at the stem, nothing at its tails), wraps the stem and runs back along both sides of the hull with its toe 1.7 m out; its height, width, push forward and opacity follow `crestLevel(speed)` (a smooth step from 1.5 to 9 m/s, so it builds when the boat speeds up and falls back as it slows, with no memory), and the foam flows back along it. It is drawn with the wake's own foam material (same shader, white water, no depth write, so the see-through hulls are as before) from the boat's live position (`WakeMesh.update(trail, now, at)`), not the trail's 12 Hz points, so it does not stutter against the hull. **Spray**: above 6 m/s (`BOW_SPRAY_FROM`) up to 32 droplets and puffs of mist (`SPRAY_COUNT`) leave the stem in short arcs, up to about 2 m high, beside the hull and trailing back; a droplet is a pure function of (index, time, speed), so there is no state. They are one mesh of camera-facing squares with a tiny shader (`bowSpray`): no particle system, no render pass. A still or slow boat has neither crest nor spray and keeps only the small ripple collar of `hull-water.ts`. The render harness has two new modes, `wake` (from 30 m behind and 13 m up) and `bow` (a low three-quarter view of the stem), and its `sp=` speed now also feeds the bow wave. **Performance pass**: per moving boat one more small mesh (51 crest vertices, 128 spray vertices, 2 more draw calls) and about 83 `waveHeight` calls on the CPU a frame; nothing per pixel beyond the foam shader the wake already used. Seen in software-rendered images only; not tried on a phone; the crest is a smooth sheet (its outer rim can show a hard polygon edge at a very low camera) and the spray reads as soft round dots, not droplets with motion.

## Blender pipeline (3D models for the web game)

Models that primitives cannot do well (people, the island props) are built in Blender by script and committed as GLB. `tools/blender` holds the code: `lib.py` (primitives, vertex-colour baking, a decimate pass that keeps the face whole, GLB export), `people.py` (five casual modern adults, `LOOKS`: bodies, heads, hair, hands and shoes lofted from cross-section rings and sculpted with smooth Gaussian bumps; clothes are coloured bands of those rings with small radius steps for hems, cuffs and waistbands, plus a shirt collar with buttons and an open zip jacket as shells; eyes have lids and lash lines, lips and brows are their own small shapes) and `build.py` (builds `public/models/people-1..5.glb`). Colours are never written twice: `npm run assets:palette` copies `src/ocean/art/colors.ts` (the people's roles are under `PAL.people`) into `tools/blender/palette.json` and a unit test fails when they drift. The exporter bakes each face's palette colour into `COLOR_0`, so a loaded model is painted like every other object (one mesh, vertex colours, the shared solid look) and has no textures; `model-loader.ts` turns off the vertex alpha so the model is drawn opaque with depth writes. In the game `src/ocean/model-loader.ts` loads a GLB lazily (the loader sits in the sea's `babylon` chunk).

`props.py` builds the island props (`palm-1..3`, `rock-1..3`, `bush-1..3`, `hut`, see "Map detail"): stylised flat-shaded low-poly pieces made with bmesh, one material per palette colour so `lib.bake_colors` writes them into `COLOR_0` (a few straw and wood shades are written in the script itself). Unlike `build.py` it never calls `lib.reset()`: it adds a NEW scene (`Props`) to the running Blender, so it can be driven through the Blender MCP without touching the open file: `import sys; sys.path.insert(0, "<repo>/tools/blender"); import importlib, props; importlib.reload(props); props.build_scene("Props"); props.render("/tmp/props.png"); props.export_glb("<repo>/public/models")`. The hut's door is Blender's +Y. Budgets (checked by `tests/props-models.test.mjs`): a palm 1,100 triangles and 5 to 9 m tall with its base at the origin, a rock 400 and under 2.5 m, the hut 600 and 3 to 6 m above its ground level with posts 0.8 m below it.

Rebuild (needs Blender as a Python module, not part of CI; the GLBs are committed): `uv venv .blender-venv --python 3.13 && uv pip install --python .blender-venv/bin/python bpy`, then `npm run assets`. Budgets: 4,800 triangles for a person's body and 500 for the waving arm (`tests/models.test.mjs` checks them, plus the nodes, colours and a 1.5-1.9 m height). The whole sea must stay under 200,000 triangles (`ocean-babylon` browser test; raised from 150,000 for the island props, looks first by the user's choice). A model's front faces -Y in Blender, which is the pier's -Z (out to sea) in the game. Limits: no textures, no skeletal animation (a waving arm is a separate pivot node), and the people are lofted and sculpted by script, so they are stylised-realistic low-poly figures rather than scanned or hand-sculpted characters. `PEOPLE_STATS=1` prints each part's triangles and `PEOPLE_RAW=1` skips the decimate pass (for previews).

## The water the boats stir: a simulation (replaces the foam buffer and the wake rings)

The wake is no longer drawn from a recorded path or a ring added by hand: it is the result of boats pushing on simulated water (`src/ocean/ripple-sim.ts`, `tests/ripple-sim.test.mjs`).

- **The grid.** 128 x 128 cells of 1 m round the camera (a torus: a point of the world always lands on the same cell, and the cells that leave the window are cleared as they come in on the other side, no copying). Each cell holds the height of the surface above the sea's own waves, its rate of rise, and white water.
- **What a hull does.** From 1.2 m/s (full at 9.5) it lifts the water ahead of its middle and lets it fall behind it, in proportion to its speed (the water it parts and refills), and its propeller churns white water behind the stern. Nothing else is put in.
- **What the water does.** The height spreads as a wave at 4.2 m/s, a little damped (0.55 per second), with a sponge at the window's rim. A boat faster than 4.2 m/s therefore leaves a V whose half angle is `asin(4.2 / speed)` (checked in the tests at 8 and 11 m/s), two wakes that cross add up (checked), a turn bends the wake (checked), and a slow boat leaves only a bump that moves with it. Steep water (slope over 0.075) breaks into white water, the white water spreads sideways (0.7 m²/s, so a wash widens as it ages) and thins out (5 s).
- **One source for the eye and the hull.** The grid is uploaded each frame as a float texture (`pack`, 128 x 128 RGBA32F: height, slope x, slope z, white water) and the sea's vertex shader displaces by it and its fragment shader tilts the normal and paints the foam from it (`glslSim`, a bilinear read done in the shader). The physics reads the same cells (`sample`) as `extraHeight`, so a boat rocks on its own and on other boats' wakes. The CPU cost is about 0.3 ms a frame (measured in Node); the GPU cost is one 128 x 128 float texture upload, four texture reads per vertex and per pixel. Gone: the 64 rings of `wake.ts`, the foam render pass (`foam-buffer*.ts`, one pass fewer) and the three trails' puffs.
- **Visual lace is still the shader's:** the grid holds only how dense the foam is; the bubbles that break it up are drawn by the same lace as the surf. The foamy bow crest sheet is no longer drawn (the grid raises the real bow wave); the spray droplets stay.
- **Limits.** The window is 128 m across and only the middle 44 to 59 m is trusted (a wake farther than that from the camera is not drawn); the boats in it are the ones the client knows of (remote boats from their played-back poses). Each client simulates for itself: two players see wakes that agree in shape but not cell for cell. A float texture is required (`OES_texture_float`/WebGL2).

**Seen** in software-rendered screenshots (render harness `wake` mode at 6 and 9 m/s). **Not measured:** the cost on a phone, and how the extra bump under the hull feels in play (about 14 cm at full speed).

### Hub shell

All four in-game hubs (Kho cá, Chợ, Thủy thủ, Cài đặt) share one shell, `src/app/hub-shell.ts`: the dialog is full screen with an 8 px margin (plus the safe-area insets), a cobalt rail of tabs on the left (76 px, tabs 44 to 56 px high) and one page beside it (`.hub-page`, 8 px × 10 px padding). A tap on a rail tab changes hub inside the same dialog (`HubNav` in `app/ocean.ts`); the Chợ tab shows only near a pier with a server economy, and the `Đóng` tab (`.fish-store-close`) closes. The shell owns the dialog's hub classes (`is-stock`, `is-market`, `is-crew-hub`, `is-settings`, `data-hub`); each hub only fills the page with its header (`.fish-store-head`), body (`.fish-store-body`) and optional foot. On portrait the rail lies along the top. The market lists each kind as one row (picture, name, heaviest, price button); the page the player left is never redrawn by a late market answer (`dialog.dataset.hub` check). Chosen by the user from two mock-ups (the paper-and-rail "Sổ tay thuyền trưởng" over the glass "Bảng lái"), 2026-11-18. Seen in desktop-Chromium screenshots of the real CSS at 844×390 and 704×337 with sample data, not on a phone and not with the live game behind it.

## The sea floor: real geometry for the whole sea

Until now the floor was only paint in the water's shader. It is now geometry (`src/ocean/seabed.ts` the rules and data, `seabed-visual.ts` the meshes, `tests/seabed.test.mjs`), seen through the clear-water layer.

- **Profile.** One depth for the whole sea, `seabedDepth(d)` (`shore.ts`, its GLSL twin in the shader, `tests/shore.test.mjs`): the island's own shelf at the sand, then from 25 m off the coast a smoothstep of 34 m more by 500 m out (`OPEN_FLOOR`): about 5 m at 60 m out, 18 m at 200 m, 42 m from 500 m, never steeper than 0.11 (a bit over 6 degrees). It was about 46 m by 170 m out before, steeper. The water's paint (`lookDepth` in `water.ts`) now uses the same depth (plus 15 % of slow variation) instead of its own invented one, so the real and painted floors meet.
- **Meshes.** The sea is cut into 128 m tiles, one mesh each (`seabed-<x>-<z>`), with a grid as fine as the tile needs (6.4 m cells within 60 m of a coast, 12.8 m to 200 m, 25.6 m to 420 m) and one coarse mesh of 64 m cells (`seabed-far`) for the deep floor between them; a skirt hangs from each tile's edge so no crack shows where grids of different fineness meet; tiles wholly inland are skipped. 388 meshes, about 257 000 triangles, built in about 0.1 s at start-up (measured in Node).
- **Relief and life.** Dunes tens of metres across, long swells of the floor in the deep, silt in the hollows, rocky outcrops, weed (and tufts of seagrass), coral heads, boulders: the patches fade out with depth because the grid under them gets coarser. About 700 coral heads, 1 500 weed tufts and 900 boulders stand within 90 m of the coasts. The floor never rises above -0.5 m past the beach (`SEABED_CEILING`): a keel and the troughs of the waves stay clear of it. It is not collision: a boat cannot run aground.
- **Drawn only on the layer.** The tiles carry a layer mask the scene's own camera does not have, so the main pass does not draw them (the opaque sea would cover them), and the clear-water layer, whose list is chosen by name and does not look at the mask, draws those within 170 m (`CLEAR.reach`). When the layer is off (slow frames, no support) the floor is the water's paint alone, as before. The tiles are not mirrored (`reflection-model.ts`).
- **Clearer water.** `CLEAR.murk` 2.4 to 1.3 and `CLEAR.see` 0.42 to 0.2 (a thing under the water is seen to about 8 to 12 m, was 2 to 4), and the paint's murk factor 0.8 to 0.55 (`tests/clear-water.test.mjs` moved with them).
- **Not measured:** the cost on a phone (388 more meshes in the scene, the layer drawing about 12 tiles within reach); how the floor looks in the real game rather than the software render (the render harness shows the clearer water and no error; the tiles themselves were checked in Blender). Review file: the scene "Seabed" in Blender is built from `scratchpad/export-seabed.mjs` and `build_seabed.py` (not in the repository).
