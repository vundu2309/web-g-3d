# Shared fishing sea

Approved 2026-11-05: each player is a separate fishing boat in one common online map. Enter through the hub's “Ra khơi” (see “Hub, sign-in and loading” below); authenticate with the existing Firebase Email/Password dialog, then sail immediately. No room creation, invitations, lobby, readiness or host. `?view=ocean` restores this direct-entry route after reload. Returning to the hub leaves the sea.

## Implementation and ownership

- `src/app/harbor.ts`: active hub, account observer, entry/exit ownership.
- `src/app/ocean.ts`: controls, fishing interaction, HUD/dialogs, animation and cleanup.
- `src/ocean/model.ts`: deterministic per-UID spawn, bounded movement, island collisions, remote payload validation.
- `src/ocean/babylon-scene.ts`: the Babylon.js scene: one engine, Havok physics for the player's boat, a chase camera, instanced boats for other players and safe text labels. It builds from `water.ts` (wave-displaced sea shader), `sky.ts` (gradient sky, sun, clouds), `boat-visual.ts` and `island-visual.ts` (procedural models merged into one vertex-coloured mesh each, so a boat is one draw call and other players' boats are instances), `havok.ts` (loads the WASM), `render-scale.ts` (resolution governor) and `sea-style.ts` (shared colours and sun).
- `src/platform/ocean.ts`: selects the same configured provider as the account layer. `ocean-firebase.ts` owns the legacy Firestore lease; `ocean-socket.ts` uses the Supabase-authenticated Render Socket.IO API. `server/ocean.mjs` owns the ephemeral shared sea for that API instance.

The original Monopoly board/HUD/artwork and room/auth services remain. `?view=game` is the original local Monopoly preview; `?view=monopoly-hub` runs its preserved room hub. New modules do not use room transitions or alter Monopoly economics/resources.

## Controls and fishing

On touch, one thumb drags the on-screen stick (`src/ocean/stick.ts`). Touching anywhere in the lower-left zone (48% wide, 66% high) makes the ring jump under the thumb and return home on release, so the thumb never has to find it; touching the ring itself keeps it where it is. There is no title on the sea screen (the “Biển chung” title and its subtitle were removed; the screen keeps `aria-label="Map biển chung"` for assistive technology). good-news notices (“Đã vào biển chung”, “Đã kết nối lại”) fade after 4 s while still being announced to screen readers, and name labels that would overlap are stacked (`separateLabels` in `src/ocean/indicator.ts`). The stick: up/down is throttle (forward/reverse) and left/right is steering, so a diagonal drag runs and turns at once; farther means stronger, a small dead zone ignores a resting thumb, and releasing or cancelling the touch stops the boat. The other thumb is free: “Thả câu” acts on press because browsers do not synthesize a click for a second finger while the first holds the stick. WASD/arrows still work as full deflection, and the stick wins while held. Fishing is described under “Fishing gameplay” below. Position, the fish store and the catch count are saved in this browser's localStorage (key `photyphu:sea:v1:<uid>`, autosave every 5 s, on each catch or release, when the tab is hidden and on leaving) and resumed on return; a save older than 7 days, outside the sea, inside an island or corrupt falls back to the harbor (the fish store is kept). The save is per device, not per account, and is not a reward, currency or anti-cheat-protected economy.

If the connection drops during play the game reconnects by itself (same boat, same place, up to 90 s, status “Mất kết nối. Đang kết nối lại…”). Revoked sessions, a second window taking over, or a full sea are not retried and show the reason with a way back. Other players see whether a boat is fishing.

The sea is drawn even while connecting or offline; boats appear once connected. `index.html` carries a small classic script: if the game has not drawn after 8 seconds it shows the failure reason with “Tải lại” and “Xóa bộ nhớ đệm và tải lại” (unregisters this app’s service worker and shell caches) instead of a blank page.

Foreground controls fit 844×390, 932×430 and 667×375; portrait falls back to stacked actions. Controls have at least 44px targets, dialog focus, keyboard operation and reduced-motion waves. Native phone performance and OS input behavior still require device verification.

**Look of the controls** (variant A, chosen by the user): icon-only glass buttons (translucent white with a blur; a plain translucent fallback where `backdrop-filter` is missing), all at least 48 px: back (arrow), the fish store (fish icon and the count) and the crew count. The fishing button is the one solid, yellow control (78 px) and its icon shows what it does: a hook (cast), an arrow thrown out (let go), a turning reel (haul in), a jolt (pull now); the words are only its accessible name (`aria-label`: Thả câu / Thả ra! / Thu cần / Giật!, the store button reads “Kho cá n”). There is no first-time hint text. On portrait phones the harbor art is shown wide enough for the whole boat and sun instead of being cropped to the middle (`.hub-art` in `style.css`); a faint seam can still be seen at the bottom edge of the art on a 390×844 screen. Seen in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390, 667×375, 390×844 and 360×640 only, not on a real phone.

## Input layer (no long-press menus)

**Minimap and crew list.** A small map of the whole sea sits in the top-right corner under the crew button (`src/app/minimap.ts` draws it, `src/ocean/minimap-math.ts` has the geometry, tested in `tests/minimap.test.mjs`): north up, the sea wall dashed, the islands with a sand ring, the channel buoys, the harbor, the other boats as arrows in their hull colours (a ring when they are fishing) and the player as a yellow arrow with a pulse that points where the boat heads. The still layer is painted once and the boats are redrawn ten times a second; it is only drawn, so no touch goes to it, and it is hidden while the loading card is up. The crew button opens a list (not a plain list of text any more): a header with the title, the number of boats and an X, then one rounded row per boat with a round avatar in its hull colour (`src/ocean/palette.ts` gives the same colour everywhere), the name (shortened with an ellipsis if long), what it is doing (green dot “Đang trên biển”, red dot “Đang câu”) and its distance in metres, you first and then the nearest. Names are put in as text, never as HTML. Checked in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390, 667×375 and 390×844.

The sea's controls (back, crew, fish store, the fishing button, the stick ring) are only **drawn**: they have `pointer-events: none`. One transparent layer (`.ocean-input`, `src/ocean/input-layer.ts`) takes every touch, finds the control under the finger by position (hit area at least 44 px even when the drawing is smaller), keeps one route per finger so the stick and the fishing button work together, and blocks `contextmenu`, `selectstart` and `dragstart`. Because a finger never lands on a button, text, icon or link, the browser has nothing to turn into a long-press menu or callout, and nothing that can cancel a held action. A touch the system cancels counts as a lift, so nothing is left held. The buttons stay real `<button>`s for the keyboard and assistive technology: their `click` still works. While the loading card is up the layer is off and the back button takes its own touches. Dialogs (crew list, fish store) are still HTML and are tap-only. This is verified in Chromium with real touch events and a synthetic `contextmenu`; it has not been tried on a real phone or in Safari.

## Fishing gameplay

Rules are pure code in `src/ocean/fishing.ts` (species, zones, the cast, the fish's behaviour, the store); the screen is `src/app/fishing-ui.ts`; the line, the rod's bend, the fish and the ripples are `src/ocean/fishing-gear.ts` (drawn through `OceanScene.setGear`). Every fish state is a signal in motion, not text.

1. **Charge and throw.** Hold “Thả câu” (or Space) and a power bar fills in 1.4 s; let go to throw. The lure lands 8 to 40 m ahead and a little to starboard (where the rod points). A dotted arc and a ring on the water preview the landing; green means the sweet spot (22 to 30 m), where fish wait less and better fish are likelier. A throw that would land on an island or outside the sea fizzles. Keyboard activation without a hold throws at the sweet spot.
2. **Nibbles.** There is no float. The line hangs slack in a curve to the water (a rope, see below). A nibble twitches the line and nods the rod tip, spreads a ring where the line enters the water and shows a blinking “!” above that spot: a fish is about to bite. A round has 1 to 3 nibbles; one fish in five loses interest and another comes later. Pulling now only reels the line in.
3. **The bite.** The line goes taut and trembles, the line is dragged under and away from the boat, the button turns into the pull, a ring around it drains and the screen edge reddens. The pull must come within **1 second** (`HOOK_WINDOW_MS`); after that the fish is gone.
4. **The catch.** The landed fish hangs from the rod tip in 3D (sized by its weight) with a small tag (rarity stars, weight, ✦ for a species record) and two drawn icon buttons: keep (✓, the fish rises away into the store) and put back (the fish falls into the sea). The fish is stored only when kept; throwing again, opening the store or leaving the sea keeps it, so a landed fish is never lost by accident. There is no card or dialog.
5. **Kho cá.** A full-screen hub (not a small dialog) with an X icon at the top right (`Đóng`, also Esc), the count held, and a grid of only the species held now: picture, name, count (`×n`), record weight with rarity stars and a put-back icon button (one fish back in the sea; the record stays). Nothing is shown for species never caught, no silhouettes. The picture of each species is its own 3D model drawn once to a small canvas by `src/app/fish-thumb.ts` (maths in `src/ocean/creature-thumb.ts`: turn, shade, sort far to near; no second WebGL context), so the 2D SVG art is gone.

The line is out (and others see the boat as fishing, the boat is not driven) from the throw until the line is in. A boat that drifts more than 48 m from where the line enters the water, a dialog, or a hidden tab reels it in. Twenty species are in the table (the original ten fish: cơm, bống, chim, nóc, mú, thu đảo, hồng, ngừ, đuối, kiếm; and ten added later: nục, cua biển, sao biển, cá hề, bơn, ngựa, mực ống, bạch tuộc, tôm hùm, mập). Each names one of the sixteen 3D models (`shape`) and one fight style; which can be caught depends on where the bobber lands: near an island shore, in open water, or far from every island (`zoneAt`). Weights are skewed to the light end of each species' range. Everything is simulated on the client with no server authority, so there is no economy, trade or reward; catching and releasing are personal. Not done yet: schools of fish visible on the sea, a fight with line tension, and other players seeing the bobber (they still see the existing short line).

### Tug of war (after the hook)

Pulling inside the one-second window now only **sets the hook** (`hooked`); the fish is landed only after a fight (`src/ocean/fight.ts`, pure rules with their own tests). From then on the button is **held to reel and released to give line**. The fish is in one of three moods: **surge** (it bursts away and takes line: reeling against it spikes the line tension and snaps the line, so let go), **strong** (it pulls steadily and surges now and then: short pulls win line and tire it, reeling too long builds tension until it snaps) and **tired** (it can be reeled in; left alone for a few seconds it recovers). The fish is landed when the distance to the boat runs out (`caught`); a snap, running out the whole line, shaking the hook after 70 s or leaving the sea (`cancel`) is `lost`. Each kind of animal fights in a style (`steady`, `runner`, `heavy`, `jet`, `crawler`, `glider`; the species table picks one) and bigger fish pull harder and last longer. A frame never advances the fight by more than 0.1 s, so a slow phone sees slow motion, not a jump.

Simulated with a bot: a careful player (reel when tired, or when strong and the line is slack) lands every style at light and heavy weights in about 9 to 22 s; holding the button always snaps the line; never reeling never lands the fish. **These numbers are first guesses, not tuned by play**: the constants at the top of `fight.ts` are meant to be adjusted by feel on a real phone. The button exposes `data-phase`, `data-mood`, `data-tension` and `data-distance` for styling and tests (never as text).

**What the fight looks like** (`src/ocean/fight-look.ts` holds the numbers, `fishing-gear.ts` draws them; no text): the line goes white, then yellow, then red as the tension climbs, hums and trembles harder near the snap and sags when the fish tires. The hooked fish is its species' 3D model at the end of the line, `distance` metres from the rod: it thrashes, swings and splashes in a surge, swims steadily when strong and lies on its side when tired. When the line snaps or the fish gets away the loose end whips back to the rod. Checked with software-rendered screenshots only (844×390): the fish is clearly visible in a surge but small and low when tired and far away; the rod does not bend yet and the button has no tension ring yet. Not verified on a real phone.

**Rod, line and the hand** (no float anywhere). The rod (`src/ocean/rod.ts` for the maths, `src/ocean/rod-visual.ts` for the mesh) is a damped spring on a tapered blank with a cork handle, a reel and a bright tip: the pull of the line bends it (more near the tip, the length stays the same), it springs back and rings after a release, and a surging fish shakes it. The tip it reports is where the line starts, so the line and the rod always agree; every boat has a rod, only the player's moves. The line (`src/ocean/rope.ts`) is a Verlet rope pinned between the rod tip and the lure or fish's mouth: slack when waiting (it hangs in a curve and rests on the sea), straight and humming when a fish pulls, and its pull on the rod comes from the fight (a surge is hard, a tired fish hardly pulls). The rope's gravity is scaled down as it tightens, since tension swamps its weight. While a fish pulls the line has no spare length at all (a few per cent of spare line sags the middle by metres: 3 % of 20 m is about 2.5 m), and it is drawn pulled onto the straight chord from the rod tip to the fish's mouth, because the simulation lags a fish that swings fast; waiting, it keeps a gentle sag of about half a metre, and a tired fish lets it curve a little. The drawn line is a flat strip that always faces the camera (`src/ocean/ribbon.ts`), thicker with distance, because a GL line is one pixel wide and vanishes; it is smoothed (`smoothRope`) so a nearly taut rope is not saw-toothed. The browser test samples the drawn line while a fish pulls and fails if its middle hangs more than half a metre off the straight line. Each ripple stays where it was made, so a running fish leaves a trail of rings. Fish are drawn larger the farther they are (about 2.5 times at 25 m) so they can be seen. A landed fish leaps from the water to hang by the mouth just below the rod tip, its length (1.7 to 3.2 m in the scene) following how heavy it is for its species.

**Device vibration** (`src/app/haptics.ts`, pure and tested with a fake `vibrate`): while the fish surges the phone buzzes without a break, with longer pulses as the line tightens; a taut line near the snap ticks as a warning; a tired fish is quiet; it stops when the fish is landed, lost or the player leaves. It needs `navigator.vibrate`: **Safari on iPhone has none, so iPhones feel nothing**; Android (Chrome) is the only place it can be felt, and I have not felt it on a real phone.

## Sea animal models

Every sea animal is built from code, like the boat: plain geometry in `src/ocean/creature-geometry.ts` and `src/ocean/creatures.ts` (no Babylon, so it is tested without a GPU: valid finite arrays, a triangle budget of 6,000 per animal, facing +X, the hook inside the body, closed bodies facing outward), and `src/ocean/creature-kit.ts` turns one into a body mesh plus a tail that swings on its own pivot, with one shared material and the colours carried by the vertices. Sixteen body plans: the eight fish shapes already in the species table (`slim`, `round`, `diamond`, `puffer`, `chunky`, `torpedo`, `flat`, `sword`) and `shark`, `flounder`, `seahorse`, `squid`, `octopus`, `crab`, `lobster`, `starfish`; the species supplies the body and fin colours (and optionally a `Look`: pattern, marking and iris colour). Fish have markings on the skin (lateral stripe, bars, spots, mottling), fins made of rays and membrane, gill covers, a mouth and layered eyes; the other animals have suckers, jointed legs, toothed claws, ribbed segments and tubercles. A ray, crab, lobster and starfish are built to show their backs to a viewer at -Z. Renders: `docs/design/sea-creatures-prototype.png` (all sixteen) and `docs/design/sea-creatures-detail-{A,B,C}.png` (larger), taken with software-rendered Chromium, not a phone. An ink outline (inverted hull) was tried and dropped: it swallowed the thin fins. They are drawn in the game: the hooked fish, the fish hanging from the rod and the store thumbnails all use them. Species balance (first guesses, not tuned by play): shore gives the small common ones (cơm, bống, cua, sao biển, nục) and some rarer (hề, bơn, ngựa, nóc, bạch tuộc, tôm hùm); open water mixes thu, hồng, mú, mực and bơn; deep water gives ngừ, đuối, kiếm, mực, bạch tuộc and the rare mập (60 to 400 kg, `heavy`).

## Map detail

The three islands and the sea around them carry more detail, all scenery: nothing here is solid and nothing is shared state (the boats still only collide with `islands` in `model.ts`, and the server is unchanged).

- **Islands** (`src/ocean/island-visual.ts`, `island-props.ts`, `geo.ts`): a terrain grid (76 × 76 over 1.4 of the radius; cells lying wholly on the flat sea floor are dropped) with patches of dry grass, ripples in the sand, a wet-sand band and a few layered rock outcrops; hundreds of grass tufts and flowers, bushes, broadleaf trees next to the palms (more palms than before), clusters of angular boulders at the shore (some in the shallows), shells and starfish, driftwood, a wooden boardwalk with rails and a lantern down to the beach, a stilted hut with a roof, a rowboat on the sand, and a door, windows and a railing on the lighthouse. The small things are written into one buffer (`Geo`) and merged with the terrain, so each island is still one mesh. Seeded: every client builds the same island. A pier into open water was tried and dropped: a pier past the ring boats cannot enter (1.08 of the radius) would be sailed through, so the boardwalk ends inside the ring (at 1.06 of the radius; since the coast now lies at 0.85 to 0.99 of the radius, its last few metres stand over the shallows like a small landing).
- **Island shape** (`islandShape` in `island-visual.ts`, pinned by `tests/map-detail.test.mjs`). The islands used to be one bell-shaped hill 0.38 to 0.56 of the radius high with a near-circular coast, and read as fake cones. They are now low green islands in the manner of Lý Sơn or Cô Tô: two or three overlapping low hills (Gaussian, 0.3 to 0.4 of the radius wide) set along a seeded ridge, plus slow undulations, scaled so the highest point is exactly `peak` = 0.20 to 0.24 of the radius (about 6.7, 8.3 and 5.5 m on the three islands); the land is never steeper than about 0.6 (31°). A sand apron rises from the waterline to about 1.2 m over the first few metres (`1.2 (1 − e^(−d/2.6))`, with d the distance inland) and the hills fade in behind it over 0.32 of the radius, so the beach is a medium band of a few metres; below the waterline the apron sinks to −1.6 m over about ten metres. The coast is not a circle: `coast(angle)` = 0.92 of the radius plus three slow harmonics (2, 3 and 5 per turn, seeded phases) and a faint crinkle, clamped to 0.85 to 0.99 of the radius, so it has bays and headlands but always stays inside the collision ring (1.08) and the piers (at the ring plus 9 m) keep their place. The lighthouse stands on the highest point found by a 0.5 m search, sunk to the lowest ground under its foot. The backdrop islands use the same recipe on a coarser grid. Seen only in software-rendered screenshots; the foam ring is still the old flat circle (the next step, shore and surf), so it now sits a few metres off the sand in places.
- **The sea** (`src/ocean/sea-layout.ts` for the data and the gull flight, `sea-props.ts` for the meshes): eight red and green channel buoys (a gate north of the harbor and a marked channel off each island) that bob and lean with the waves, seven seagulls circling the islands (instances of one small mesh), and five coarse backdrop islands beyond the edge of the sea (`SEA_BOUND`), seen through the haze and unreachable. Buoys are not solid, so a boat can sail through one.
- **Cost.** Measured in a software-rendered browser: the active triangles were about 80 000 at the harbor (every island and the backdrop in view), 40 000 to 54 000 at an island, against about 44 000 before. `tests/map-detail.test.mjs` runs headless (no GPU) and holds each island between 9 000 and 26 000 triangles and a backdrop island under 6 500. **The frame rate on a phone has not been measured**; if it is too slow, the grass, flowers and backdrop are what to cut first.
- Seen only in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390.

## Online contract and limits

On the Firebase configuration, all users query the same `monopolyHub/v1/oceanBoats` collection ordered by `updatedAt` descending, limited to 100 most recently updated boats. No silent offline/demo fallback or sample players. Identity is a UID-keyed document; display name contains no account email. A new tab for the same account replaces its old session, which stops controlling the boat. Leave deletes only a matching lease; abrupt loss expires visually within 30 seconds. Hidden tabs stop motion/heartbeats and can expire remotely; foreground entry resumes updates. Deletion is best effort on abrupt tab closure.

Transactions fail offline rather than queue old motion. Motion writes on the Firebase adapter are at most 1 Hz, idle heartbeats at 10 seconds, remote render positions are interpolated. Firestore read/write volume grows with active boats and is unsuitable for a large continuous-action world without an authoritative realtime server. Coordinates and ownership are validated by prototype rules, but movement/fishing remain client-simulated. There is no persistent catch economy, trading, chat, matchmaking or server anti-cheat. Production backend permissions must be deployed separately; the frontend does not grant access by itself.

On the Supabase configuration, the existing account and password-recovery flow are preserved. All sea clients enter the `ocean:shared` Socket.IO channel on the existing API; user identity comes from server-validated Auth, never a payload UID. Joining validates the token once, enforces one active session per UID, validates finite bounded pose fields, bounds payload/rate and caps the sea at 100 boats. Joined sea clients alone receive pushes. Disconnect removes the matching boat; old replaced sockets cannot remove the new boat. No polling or Supabase schema writes are introduced.

### Transport (protocol 2)

`server/ocean.mjs` relays; clients still simulate their own boat. Measured with 50 simulated boats it cut one client's inbound traffic from about 350 KB/s to 6.6 KB/s while updates became five times as frequent, and moves stopped calling Supabase (about 44 calls/s before).

- **Moves are synchronous.** `ocean:move` (client to server, no acknowledgement, volatile) is an in-memory write, validated and rate limited (100 ms per socket). Too-fast or invalid moves are dropped silently; losing the lease is reported with `ocean:error`.
- **One tick, deltas only.** A fixed tick (200 ms, only when something changed) sends `ocean:delta` `{t, add:[{uid,name,x,z,heading,fishing,updatedAt}], up:[[uid,x,z,heading,fishing 0/1,movedAt]], gone:[uid]}`. A joiner first gets one full `ocean:state`. Entering and leaving are flushed at once.
- **Session checks on a timer.** The session is re-validated every 60 s per sea socket (`authRecheckMs`). A rejected token ends the connection with `ocean:error` "Hãy đăng nhập lại."; the Auth provider being unreachable never does (`authenticate` throws on 5xx, returns null only on 401/403). The client sends a renewed access token on the open connection with `ocean:token`, so a session is not cut when the first token (about one hour) expires. If a server without this handler cuts the connection, the client reconnects once with a fresh token; only a refused handshake means signing in again.
- **Playback, not chasing.** Clients keep a short history per remote boat (`src/ocean/remote.ts`) and draw it about 500 ms in the past (2.5 ticks: the sender's period, the wait for the next tick and jitter), interpolating between samples stamped with when the boat moved, not with the tick that carried them. Simulated with 60 ms of jitter and a sender whose pace drifts against the tick, a 300 ms trail left about a fifth of the frames standing still and 450 ms none (`tests/ocean.test.mjs` pins this); `ServerClock` follows the fastest recent path so jitter does not move playback. The renderer is unchanged.
- **Compatibility.** The client announces `proto: 2` when joining; the server answers with `proto`, `sendMs` and `delayMs`. A server without it keeps the old behaviour (acknowledged `ocean:command` moves, full `ocean:state` snapshots, 1 Hz, 1.3 s playback delay); a client without it keeps getting full snapshots. Deploy order does not matter.
- **Not done:** interest management (sending only nearby boats) and binary encoding; with at most 100 boats at 5 Hz they are not needed yet. The server is still one instance with in-memory state.

This Socket.IO sea is intentionally ephemeral and requires exactly **one API process/instance**: restart/redeploy clears boats and players re-enter; positions and catches are not saved. Do not scale Render to multiple instances until shared authoritative world state/broadcast is implemented. Existing PostgreSQL LISTEN/NOTIFY continues serving the preserved room hub; it is not falsely presented as synchronizing the ephemeral sea across instances. The adapter fails visibly and returns to the hub on connection failure instead of switching providers.

## Boat physics (Babylon.js + Havok)

See [decision 0008](decisions/0008-babylon-havok-boat-physics.md). `src/ocean/boat-physics.ts` runs the hull as a Havok rigid body and adds, as per-step impulses, buoyancy at six hull-bottom points against `src/ocean/waves.ts`, quadratic water drag that is far stronger across the keel than along it, propeller thrust, and a rudder whose authority grows with speed. Islands are static cylinder colliders (`islandCollisionRadius`, just past the visible beach), and a soft wall keeps the boat inside the sea the server accepts. The player's boat is Havok's; the controller reads its pose each frame (clamped to the sea bounds) and publishes `x`, `z`, `heading`, `fishing` exactly as before, so the server contract did not change. Other players' boats are not simulated: they follow published poses and rest on the wave surface under them. Steering is analog, so the touch stick maps straight onto throttle and rudder; while casting the boat gets no thrust and drag brings it to rest.

The world uses right-handed axes (`enableSeaPhysics`), so heading 0 is -Z and a positive heading swings the bow toward +X, matching the movement model and the published poses. Havok steps at a fixed 60 Hz regardless of frame rate, and a frame longer than 100 ms is clamped, so a slow phone sees slow motion rather than a jump.

**Speed and parting the water** (`src/ocean/boat-physics.ts`, `src/ocean/wake.ts`). The boat is faster: thrust 6 000 N and a top speed of about 12 m/s (it was 8; the movement model's `BOAT_SPEED` follows), about 10 m/s after three seconds. From the speed along the keel the hull now behaves like one pushing a bow wave: a hump of extra wave-making drag around 7 m/s where the bow climbs (about 5 to 8 degrees of bow-up pitch while accelerating), then from 6 to 10.5 m/s it rides up on the water (a lift at the centre, 15 % less forward drag, the bow levels out), and it leans into a turn at speed (roll about the keel from steer and speed; the other way for the other turn). A slow boat is as before. The wake is a visual of the water only, in two parts. **The swell**: each moving boat (yours and the others', whose speed comes from their played-back poses) drops a ring of wave every 0.18 s; the ring spreads at 4.2 m/s, widens and fades, and the rings along a path add up to a V whose half angle is `asin(4.2 / speed)`, narrow for a fast boat and wide for a slow one, with no angle written anywhere. The water vertex shader sums up to 64 rings (`glslWake`, the CPU twin `sampleWake`, the slope checked against a finite difference in `tests/wake.test.mjs`) and adds a low swell (12 cm at most) to the water. **The white water** (`src/ocean/wake-trail.ts` for the geometry, `wake-mesh.ts` to draw it, `tests/wake-trail.test.mjs`): each boat records its path every 0.08 s and three strips are laid on the sea along it, a camera-facing-flat ribbon each: the wash behind the stern, as wide as the hull at first and widening as it ages, and a thin arm to each side that runs back from the bow and spreads at the wave speed. They follow the recorded path, so they bend in a turn. The foam is a tiling noise texture (`foamPixels`: streaks along the path with a few bubbles) whose coordinate along the strip is the distance travelled, so it stays where it was made instead of sliding with the boat; the shader cuts the foam away where a strip is faint, so it breaks into patches and dissolves rather than fading like smoke, and fades with distance. The first version drew flat soft white discs behind the stern and took the foam from the sparse vertices of the sea grid, which looked like blobs and was rejected by the user; those are gone. It does not push the boats: they float on `waves.ts`, and the foam lies 16 cm above the waves. Seen in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390 (thin white arms from the stern at full speed, curved in a turn); the frame cost on a phone of the 64-ring sum in the vertex shader and of one small mesh per moving boat has not been measured.

**The sea surface** (`src/ocean/waves.ts`, `src/ocean/water.ts`). Twelve travelling waves (61 m swell down to 2.2 m chop, amplitudes summing to 0.825 m) with deep-water dispersion, headed in a band around one wind (`WIND_DIRECTION`): the long swell within about 0.3 rad of it, the short chop spread up to about 1.5 rad either side, so neighbouring waves beat into wave groups instead of a uniform corrugation, each a sharpened sine (`((1 + sin) / 2)^sharp`, normalised to a zero mean) so crests are narrow and troughs broad like a real sea. The height is still a pure vertical function of (x, z, t), identical on the CPU (buoyancy, remote boats, the fishing line) and in the vertex shader (generated from the same table), and bounded by the summed amplitudes. The slope is analytic (`waveGradient`, and `waveSlope` in GLSL, checked against a central difference in `tests/boat-physics.test.mjs`), with each wave's slope faded out with distance in proportion to its wavelength and skipped once it is zero, so the far sea only pays for the long swell. The fragment shader adds fine ripples (purely visual: they tilt the normal, never the geometry the boats float on) from a 128 × 128 tileable slope map generated at startup (`src/ocean/ripples.ts`: about 56 sines with whole-number wave vectors in a band around the wind; RG is the slope, BA the slope a quarter period later, so mixing them by cos/sin of time moves each ripple along its own direction). It is read twice per pixel, 6.7 m and 2.3 m tiles turned to either side of the wind and drifting differently (faded out by about 260 m and 110 m; mipmaps flatten it further away), and scaled by a very low-frequency value-noise field: wide calm and rough patches drifting downwind and faint streaks along the wind. The same field scales the distance roughness, so calm patches stay glassy far out and rough ones go matt. Then light through the crests, a sun glitter that widens with distance, white caps on steep crests, and water that turns turquoise with a surf line near the three islands (`islands` uniform). Seen only in software-rendered screenshots at 844×390; **the frame cost on a phone has not been measured** (an in-browser software-render timing did not synchronise the GPU and was discarded). Counting instructions only, the new shader is about as costly as the old one where the far-sea skips apply and costlier near the camera.

**Sharpness.** The sea draws at up to 2x the CSS size. `ScaleGovernor` lowers the pixel count only after two consecutive slow windows (ignoring the first 4 s), raises it again after five fast ones, and never reacts to the 30 fps cap itself. An earlier version treated the cap as "slow" and blurred the sea permanently; `tests/ocean.test.mjs` pins that behaviour.

**Cost.** Measured in the browser test (before the map detail below): about 44k triangles and 10 meshes per frame. The Babylon chunk is 1.73 MB (409 KB gzip) and the Havok WASM 2.09 MB (658 KB gzip). Both load only when entering the sea and are excluded from the service-worker precache (`scripts/pwa-build.mjs`): the sea is online-only. Frame rate on real phones has not been measured.

`tests/boat-physics.test.mjs` drives real Havok headlessly (Babylon `NullEngine`, fixed 1/60 s steps) and checks floating on the local surface, bobbing, bounded roll and pitch, gradual acceleration and coasting, turning both ways with limited sideslip, island blocking without tunnelling, the start heading, exact repeatability and the boundary wall. Removing buoyancy, drag or the island colliders makes the matching tests fail. It runs with `tests/support/register-resolve.mjs`, which only teaches Node to resolve Babylon's extensionless deep imports. `tests/ocean-babylon-browser.mjs` runs the real scene in a browser with a fake connection: 2x draw size on a 3x screen, frame cost, labels, floating, driving and turning with one touch, and a second thumb casting while the first holds the stick.

Three separate screens, so the game is not a web page with a game inside it.

## Loading and connecting

Entering the sea shows the loading screen above, with three steps (graphics, connection, entry). Graphics and the network start together, and `src/platform/wake.ts` nudges a sleeping API as soon as the home screen shows, so a free Render instance (30-60 s to wake) wakes while the engine downloads. The old flow gave up after a fixed 15 s and asked the player to go back to the hub.

`src/ocean/connect.ts` retries instead: pauses of 1.5, 2.5 then 4 s, each attempt abandoned after 25 s, a total patience of 100 s, and a "the server is waking up" note after 5 s. A connection that arrives after its attempt timed out is closed rather than leaked. Errors retrying cannot fix (signed out, opened in another window, sea full, not configured) are shown at once. If graphics take over 60 s or connecting runs out of patience, the card becomes a failure message with a **Thử lại** button (reload, which re-enters the sea directly) and the HUD's **Về hub** stays reachable above it. The same card appears if the connection drops mid-game.

Tests: `tests/ocean.test.mjs` pins the retry policy; `tests/ocean-loading-browser.mjs` runs the card in a browser with a flaky, a silent and a fatal fake server. Not measured: how long a real Render cold start takes from a phone, or the real cost of the wake-up ping.

## Finding each other

Players used to start up to 40 m apart facing random directions, so the other boat was usually behind or beside the camera and nothing said where it was: two real accounts started 27.6 m apart with one 86 degrees off the other's bow, and the data was fine ("2 thuyền") while neither could see the other.

- **One harbor.** `spawn()` (`src/ocean/model.ts`) puts everyone in a small area at `HARBOR` (8 slots plus jitter), all heading 0 toward the open sea, so any two players start within about 25 m and in front of each other. The client uses it for its own start pose and announces it about 0.5 s after entering, so others see the real spot. The Render API still assigns its own join pose until it is redeployed from this code; the client's pose wins as soon as it publishes.
- **Arrows.** `src/ocean/indicator.ts` places, for any remote boat that is outside the view, an arrow on a ring inside the screen pointing toward it with their name and distance (`.ocean-boat-label[data-edge]`). Boats in view keep a plain name label, hidden beyond 75 m.
- **Expired sessions.** The server reports why a socket was refused; `AUTH_REQUIRED` now becomes "Phiên đăng nhập đã hết hạn. Hãy đăng nhập lại." (not retried) and the failure card offers **Đăng nhập lại** (sign out, back to the home screen). Before this, a revoked session was retried every 5 s for 100 s.
- **One boat per account.** A second window of the same account takes over and the first is told so; to test with two players on one device use a private window (separate storage), or two accounts on two devices.
- A server "too fast" reply to a pose update no longer ends the session.

Tests: `tests/ocean.test.mjs` (harbor and arrow maths) and `tests/ocean-multiplayer-browser.mjs` (two real clients through the real server, an arrow with distance when out of view, a revoked session, same-account takeover; only Supabase Auth is faked). Replacing the new start with the old random one, or dropping the expired-session handling, makes that test fail.

### Fishing camera, fisherman, bigger islands, calmer sea

- While fishing (charging, flying, waiting, fighting) the camera moves closer and lower with a narrower view, and looks toward the end of the line (the lure, or the fish in a fight); it eases back to the chase camera afterwards. The line strip is a little wider and a small orange lure marks its end (the fish takes its place in a fight).
- The fish swims toward where it wants to be (no jumps between moods), faces the way it moves and floats on the surface. The line is nearly straight while waiting and with a tired fish (sag about 0.05–0.16 m).
- A fisherman (yellow coat, straw hat) stands at the stern holding the rod, part of the merged boat mesh.
- The minimap is larger (156 px; 136 px on short phones) with curved shores, shallows, palms, a lighthouse mark and a 100 m scale bar.
- Islands are about 1.7 times bigger (radii 31, 39, 26 m); props per island are capped to keep each island under the phone triangle budget. Channel buoys moved off the larger islands.
- Wave amplitudes are about 40 % lower. The wake height is added to the water the hulls float on (`extraHeight` in boat physics), so a boat rocks on the wake of another.

### Art system (graphics refactor, step 1: one palette)

All object colours of the sea game live in `src/ocean/art/colors.ts` (`PAL`, by role: wood, paint, stone, sand, plants, crew, rod, ...). Visual files take colours from it and may not write a hex of their own; `tests/art-palette.test.mjs` fails if a migrated file does, or if the palette lists one colour under two names. Near-duplicate colours that had drifted apart (three reds, two rock greys, two lamp yellows, two cream whites) were merged, so a few shades moved by a hair. Remaining files to migrate: creatures, fishing gear, water/sky/fog constants. Next steps of the refactor: one shared material factory, then shared shape helpers, then the light and shading rules applied centrally so every object follows them.

### Art system, steps 2 and 3: shared materials and shapes

- `src/ocean/art/materials.ts`: the two kinds of material objects may use. A SOLID material (vertex colours lit by the scene, with a look: `matte`, `satin`, `gloss`, `buoy`, `gull`), one shared per look and scene via `solidMaterial` / `useSolid`; and a GLOW material (unlit flat colour or texture, `glowMaterial`) for the fishing line, lure, rings and foam. Water, sky and wake stay as their own shaders.
- `src/ocean/art/shapes.ts`: `Parts` builds an object out of coloured primitives (`box`, `tube`, `sphere`, `torus`, `add` for custom meshes) and merges it into one mesh; `SIDES` gives the roundness tiers (`thin`, `small`, `round`, `smooth`); `cylinderMesh` / `sphereMesh` make bare primitives. The boat, the buoys, the rod holder, the lighthouse and the lure use them.
- `tests/art-palette.test.mjs` also fails when a visual file builds its own `StandardMaterial`/`PBRMaterial` or calls `MeshBuilder.CreateBox/Cylinder/Sphere/Torus` itself. Creatures (their own geometry in `creature-geometry.ts`) and the water/sky/wake shaders are not moved yet. Next: the light and shading rules, applied once in the solid and glow materials.

### Looking around the boat

Dragging a finger (or the mouse) on open sea turns the camera around the boat and tips it higher or lower (`src/ocean/orbit-camera.ts`, pure and tested; routed by the input layer's `look` option, so the stick, buttons and fishing still win where they are). The view stays where it is left while the boat sits still; once the boat moves (above 1.2 m/s) and no finger is down, it eases back to the default view behind the boat in about 3 seconds. From the side or the front the camera looks at the boat rather than far ahead of it. Pitch is limited (-0.3 to +0.8 rad) and the camera never goes under the water.

**Chase camera framing** (`src/ocean/chase-camera.ts`, pure and tested; applied by `followWith` in `babylon-scene.ts`). The default view is 17.5 m behind the boat and 9.6 m high, looking 34 m ahead (it was 14.5 m, 6.8 m and 30 m, which showed little of the sea); while fishing it eases to 12.5 m and 6.4 m as before. Three things keep it useful: it **keeps clear of islands and piers** (`clearFraction` finds where the line from the boat to the camera first meets the shore circle or a pier plus a 2.5 m margin, the camera comes in to there, quickly, never closer than 3.5 m and lower the closer it is, and goes back out slowly; `pushOut` then moves the camera out of any circle it still drifted into, because it follows with a little lag), it **looks into a turn** (`turnLead`: the look point swings up to 0.5 rad toward the way the boat is turning and straightens when it goes straight, and fades out while fishing), and the finger look-around above works on top of it. `tests/chase-camera.test.mjs` pins the numbers, the avoidance (including 4,000 random boats on the real sea) and the lead; not yet seen on a phone.

### Fish size

A fish is drawn at its species' real size (`drawnLength` in `src/ocean/fishing.ts`: length in cm at 1 kg times the cube root of its weight, squeezed into 0.4 to 1.5 m), for the fish on the line and the one hanging from the rod. During a fight a fish far from the camera is drawn larger so it stays visible (about 4 degrees of the view, at most 3.5 times its size). It used to be 1.7 to 3.2 m for every species, an anchovy as long as a tuna.

### Art system, step 4: light and shading, once

- `src/ocean/art/lighting.ts`: the only light rig (`createSeaLights`): a warm sun from the front left and a sky fill whose underside is the blue bounce of the sea, plus the rim-light values. Objects never create lights (a test checks).
- `src/ocean/art/toon.ts`: soft cel shading (a partly applied posterize, 5 steps, amount 0.55) added by `solidMaterial` to every solid look, so hulls, hills, props and fish read as bands of colour. The solid materials also get a cool rim glow on edges that turn away from the viewer (`LIGHT.rim`). Water, sky, wake and the unlit glow materials are not touched.
- Tuning is in the constants of these two files, not in the objects.

### Proportions: line and fish against a 7.4 m boat

The fishing line is a flat strip about two pixels wide on a phone at any distance (`0.0035` m per metre of distance, at least 1.2 cm; was about 4 px, a tape). The lure is 14 cm. A fish is drawn at its real size from its species length and weight (`drawnLength`: an anchovy a hand long, a tuna as long as a person, a shark up to 4 m, never under 0.3 m); a far fish in a fight is drawn up to 3 times larger (at most 2.2 m) so it is not a speck, and shrinks to its real size as it is reeled in. A soft dark patch on the water under the hooked fish helps to find it. The hanging catch is drawn at real size (at least 0.45 m).

### Coins (server economy, step 1: the database)

Coins ("xu") are earned by selling fish and kept on the server: wallets, a ledger of unsold caught fish and a price table in PostgreSQL, reached only through three authenticated RPCs (`economy_state`, `economy_record_catch`, `economy_sell`). The decision, its limits (a catch is still a client report) and the plan are in [decision 0010](decisions/0010-server-economy.md). `src/ocean/economy.ts` holds the same price rules as the database (`priceOf`: base price from rarity and species weight, 0.6 to 1.5 times by the fish's weight) and `tests/economy.test.mjs` fails if the migration's price table differs. `tests/economy-postgres.sh` runs the SQL scenarios (not signed in, private tables, prices, catch rules, selling, another player's fish, the 200-fish cap) against a disposable PostgreSQL. The migration is `supabase/migrations/20261113032223_economy.sql`; it was applied to the live Supabase project and `VITE_ECONOMY=on` set on Vercel production on 2026-11-13 (see the decision).

### Hub art drawn on a canvas

The picture behind the hub, the loading screen and the sign-in dialog is drawn on a `<canvas>` by `src/ui/hub-canvas.ts` (it replaces the SVG of `hub-art.ts` and its CSS animations). `artLayout` fits the 844 x 390 board to the canvas (landscape: cover, bottom-anchored; portrait: wide enough for the whole boat and sun, sky and deep-sea bands above and below) and is unit tested; `drawArt` paints one frame for a time: the violet sky with twinkling stars, the breathing sun and its glints on the water, drifting clouds, two gulls beating their wings, four waves drifting at their own speeds and the boat rolling on the swell. `mountHubArt` animates it at about 30 frames a second at most (device pixel ratio capped at 2), pauses while the page is hidden, draws a single still frame under `prefers-reduced-motion`, and returns the function that stops it (the loading screen stops it once the sea is shown). `hubArtUrl` renders a still frame for the sign-in dialog's background.

### Markets (selling fish)

Each island has a fish market: a floating wooden pier with a striped-awning stall, a gold-coin sign and a flag, on the water off the side that faces the harbor (`src/ocean/market.ts` positions, `src/ocean/market-visual.ts` the 3D pier; Chợ Hải Đăng, Chợ Đồi Xanh, Chợ Bãi Cọ). A pier is solid for boats (radius 3.4 m, added to the island colliders) and a saved boat position near one falls back to the harbor like near an island. The minimap shows each market as a gold coin with a fish. When the boat is within 17 m of a pier (not while fishing) a glass **Bán** button appears next to the fish store button; tapping it opens the market hub (`src/app/market-ui.ts`): a full-screen list of the fish the server's ledger holds, grouped by species with what each group pays, a sell button for each group, **Bán tất cả** and the wallet in xu, with an X to close. Selling asks the server (`src/platform/economy.ts`, `economy_sell`) and the fish also leave the store on this device. A kept fish is reported to the server when it is kept (`economy_record_catch`, retried when the hub opens if the network failed). The market exists only when an economy is configured: the Supabase backend with the public switch `VITE_ECONOMY=on` (it stays off until the migration in `supabase/pending/` is applied and approved); with none, no button and no hub appear. `src/ocean/economy-api.ts` holds the shared types and an in-memory economy that follows the database's rules, used by `tests/ocean-market-browser.mjs` and `tests/economy-client.test.mjs`.

## Sea light model

Sky and water share `src/ocean/sky-model.ts`: one linear-light radiance function (horizon→zenith gradient, warm glow and Mie halo toward the sun, sun disc) and one ACES tone map + gamma. The water fragment (`water.ts`) is physically based: Schlick Fresnel (F0 0.02) softened by roughness, a GGX/Smith sun specular whose roughness grows with distance, reflection of the same sky the dome draws, Beer–Lambert absorption by depth (shallows near islands show the sandy bottom), subsurface glow on crests against the sun, foam lit by sun and sky, and aerial-perspective fog tinted by the sky in the view direction. Boats, markets and islands still use Babylon's Standard materials in gamma space; there are no cast shadows yet.

### Boat shadows on the water

`water.ts` darkens the sea in the shadow of every boat (yours first, then the fleet, up to `BOAT_MAX` = 12). There is no shadow map: each boat is three slices (hull, cabin, mast), each projected onto the water along the sun's ground direction by `height / tan(elevation)`, so the low sun throws a long shadow away from it (`SUN_DIRECTION` in `sea-style.ts`). In shadow the water loses the sun's share of its light (body, glitter, foam) and a little of the mirrored sky. Boats do not shadow islands or each other's decks, and islands/markets cast no shadow on the sea.

## Market vendors and the people of the sea

Each fish market has a vendor (`vendor` on `Market` in `market.ts`: a name, a greeting and `look`, a key of `NPC_LOOKS`): Cô Lan (Hải Đăng, `lan`: long black hair under a conical hat, cream ao ba ba, teal apron), Chú Tư (Đồi Xanh, `tu`: dark-skinned, navy cap, fisherman's shirt) and Bà Sáu (Bãi Cọ, `sau`: brown hair under a coral scarf, maroon ao ba ba, rose apron). `em` (lilac ao ba ba, sky apron) and `ngudan` (mustard shirt, blue cap) are defined for later NPCs. They stand on the pier deck in front of the counter, facing the sea (the figure faces +Z; the market turns it half round), breathe slowly and wave the arm at +x about two seconds in every eight (the phase differs per market). When the market hub opens, the vendor's name and greeting show under the title. Vendors are decoration and flavour only: no gameplay effect, no server state, no chat.

The figures are small cartoon people (big round head, short body) built in code, not loaded from a file: `npc-look.ts` is plain data and maths (the looks, whose colours are only roles of the palette `PAL.people`, and the poses `idlePose`, `wavePose` and `walkPose`), `npc-visual.ts` builds a figure of six meshes (body, head, two arms, two legs), each hung from a joint node so it can swing, with the sea's shared satin solid look and one draw call per part. The parts overlap at the joints (a ball at each shoulder and hip inside the pelvis and torso, a neck under the head), so nothing floats apart; a figure is about 3,500 triangles, because Babylon's sphere with `segments` n has (n + 2) x 2(n + 2) quads and the small balls are therefore coarse (`FINE`, `COARSE`, `TINY` in `npc-visual.ts`). Limits: the joints are rigid (no smooth bending), a figure can wave, stand and walk but not sit or cast yet, and nothing walks anywhere (no NPC boats or walkers; see the deferred work). `tests/npc.test.mjs` pins the looks (palette colours only, all different), the joint layout and the pose maths; the rendering was checked in a Babylon test scene, not on a phone.

The five people built in Blender (`public/models/people-1..5.glb`, `tools/blender`, see "Blender pipeline" below) are no longer used by the vendors; they stay in the repository and are still checked by `tests/models.test.mjs`.

## Blender pipeline (3D models for the web game)

Models that primitives cannot do well (people) are built in Blender by script and committed as GLB. `tools/blender` holds the code: `lib.py` (primitives, vertex-colour baking, a decimate pass that keeps the face whole, GLB export), `people.py` (five casual modern adults, `LOOKS`: bodies, heads, hair, hands and shoes lofted from cross-section rings and sculpted with smooth Gaussian bumps; clothes are coloured bands of those rings with small radius steps for hems, cuffs and waistbands, plus a shirt collar with buttons and an open zip jacket as shells; eyes have lids and lash lines, lips and brows are their own small shapes) and `build.py` (builds `public/models/people-1..5.glb`). Colours are never written twice: `npm run assets:palette` copies `src/ocean/art/colors.ts` (the people's roles are under `PAL.people`) into `tools/blender/palette.json` and a unit test fails when they drift. The exporter bakes each face's palette colour into `COLOR_0`, so a loaded model is painted like every other object (one mesh, vertex colours, the shared solid look) and has no textures; `model-loader.ts` turns off the vertex alpha so the model is drawn opaque with depth writes. In the game `src/ocean/model-loader.ts` loads a GLB lazily (the loader sits in the sea's `babylon` chunk).

Rebuild (needs Blender as a Python module, not part of CI; the GLBs are committed): `uv venv .blender-venv --python 3.13 && uv pip install --python .blender-venv/bin/python bpy`, then `npm run assets`. Budgets: 4,800 triangles for a person's body and 500 for the waving arm (`tests/models.test.mjs` checks them, plus the nodes, colours and a 1.5-1.9 m height). The whole sea must stay under 150,000 triangles (`ocean-babylon` browser test). A model's front faces -Y in Blender, which is the pier's -Z (out to sea) in the game. Limits: no textures, no skeletal animation (a waving arm is a separate pivot node), and the people are lofted and sculpted by script, so they are stylised-realistic low-poly figures rather than scanned or hand-sculpted characters. `PEOPLE_STATS=1` prints each part's triangles and `PEOPLE_RAW=1` skips the decimate pass (for previews).

### Village outfits and the cute warp

The people keep the reference line-up's body shapes and proportions but dress for the game, not the street: an ao ba ba shirt (cream, lilac, mustard, maroon), black or brown work trousers, sandals, an apron (teal or rose) and headwear (a conical non la, a flat cap or a knotted head scarf). Outfits are plain data in `LOOKS` in `tools/blender/people.py` (`top_col`, `bottom_col`, `shoe_col`, `hat`, `hat_col`, `apron`; colours are palette roles under `PAL.people.clothes`). `cute_warp` then makes everybody a little rounder and shorter with a bigger head (`CUTE`), and the hats and apron are protected from the decimate pass because thin shells tear when simplified.
